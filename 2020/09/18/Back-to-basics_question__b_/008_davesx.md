So here is another perspective. Death, signifies the transient and unstable nature of the nāma-rūpa aggregates as an event occurring at each moment. One set of nāma-rūpa aggregates ceases as the conditions change and another set of aggregates arises. In either sense, death is only part of the law of impermanence, and it does not represent an abrupt interruption or annihilation of the One Life.

Thus death— or the end of life —is a form of change that is not radically unlike the kind of change that takes place for everything at each moment. Both nāma and rūpa die together during death, and another nāma-rūpa is born. But the two are not the same nāma-rūpa, nor are they entirely separate. They are related in that they are part of a continuum of life.  

  

By one nāma-rūpa actions are performed and by these actions another nāma-rūpa springs up. One nāma-rūpa finds its end in death but the rotting corpse or carbon ash if cremated, becomes nutrients for microscopic life forms which through the food chain causing another fresh and intelligent nāma-rūpa to emerge and the recycling of life continues indefinitely. Surely this harsh reality is no consolation for the self-identity or present nāma-rūpa which imagines it is separate or isolated from the flow of life eternal.
