In my usage a-\>b denoted direction rather than implication. We say "fire causes smoke" without obligating ourselves to a clarification "but not always." It is understood that "causes" does not necessarily imply "always", though technically the word usage can be argued that it does unless we clarify "but not always". Likewise a-\>b in my usage just meant "a then b" (implicitly "where b is affirmed") or "a leads to b" as a sequence in observed knowledge, or a causes b/ a is cause of b, as a representation for our common language understanding "fire causes smoke". I guess regular language. (If I was writing to ordinary folk, fire -\> smoke will make sense to them as it fits with their regular language usage for the same meaning. That is the idea.)

  

Also:I did not mean a-\>b as a statement of inference/vyapti but as representing the perceived world directly, since that is the ground for non-perceptual knowledge.In general what do we perceive when we can see both fire and smoke throughout? We see fire; we see smoke later. We know or see fire causing smoke. This is *ordinary* perception of instances; for most of us, that is the basis for deducing the vyapti: smoke is always caused by fire. Or smoke =\> fire; if there is smoke, then there is fire (that causes it). This vyapti is knowledge, however the basisfor that knowledge is perception of instances where the sequence being affirmed is fire then smoke, or fire -\> smoke.

  

So, I think there is room for discussion in this matter forcontextual understanding,at least in Vedanta circles where we are borrowing symbolism and adapting for our purpose. But I can see that my phrase like "knowledge of a-\>b" can be confusing if taken for mathematicalprecision.

  

Your explanation of C is very informative. I will have to think about it more including how C is inference and how it works for arthapatti in different examples. Thanks.
