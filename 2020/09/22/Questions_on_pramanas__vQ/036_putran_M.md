I also find it difficult about pinpointing the distinction of these other pramanas from anumana.

  

With arthapatti, I think there is something that sometimes makes it clear. For anumana, we must have prior observation through pratyaksha that forms the basis for our deduction of vyapti. For arthapatti, our postulation occurs as the only viableexplanation for certain observations; the object of explanation can be something without any prior instantiation through pratyaksha.

  

I was explaining this point (below) in another group which led me to the question I posted here, regarding the type of valid basis in which arthapatti has to be grounded.

  

Quote

  

Consider the following three sequences:

  

1\. Patient takes drug. -\> Drug resolves the cause of sickness in human body. (A -\> B)

  

2\. Drug resolves cause -\> Patient is cured. (B -\> C)

  

2\. Patient takes drug. -\> Patient is cured.  (A -\> C)

  

We know from perception the following: A, C and the fact that C followed A.

  

We want to know: Is B true?

  

We do not know B from perception.

  

Perhaps we know B in other contexts (like in test-tube or chemical analysis); but we have other examples where a drug worksin test-tubes but patient is not cured. Therefore we cannot use comparison (upamana) to conclude B is true in the human body because it is true in the test-tube. So we cannot use upamana to decide that B is true. (We need at the least perception of B in some human before we can use comparison based on the similarity of the workings of all human bodies.)

  

Anumana or inference also requires a perception-basis to deduce vyapti. That is, if in our perceived world, we saw B is true in human body wherever we observe it; then we can*postulate*that the vyapti hypothesis is true (the drug resolves the cause in human bodies) based on our perceived world. But here, we come to the same problem as above with comparison. We have never perceived the drug's working in the human body. So inference is not possible.

  

Now we are left with perception of A, C and the sequence A -\> C.

  

Given whatever perceptual knowledge we have (of A -\> C and of our understanding of B in other contexts), (suppose) the only explanation possible is A -\> B -\> C, that is, the Drug worked correctly in the body and therefore the patient is cured. We therefore POSTULATE (arthapatti) that B is true because A -\> C is true.

  

Note 1: This is the ideal situation where A-\>C is always true. If such were the case, we need not bring in B at all; instead we can say "Drug works" if A-\>C is always true. IF taking a herb resolves headache, then the herb works. But when our research involves study of B to find the drug, naturally we want to think A-\>C because B is true.

  

Note 2: In truth there can be other extraneous reasons why C follows A (or not) whether B is true or not.The model system involving clinical trials and rules like the 5% rule above allows arthapatti to operate in an axiomatic probabilistic framework.

  

Note 3:In either case, when we talk of a non-perceptual event like B based on perceptions of A and C, we are resorting to arthapatti of some kind. 

  

  

Unquote
