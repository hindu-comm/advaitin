> The way that I understand Anumana is to come to a conclusion based on
> the gathered facts. For example seeing the smoke, we can infer that
> there could be a fire. But this is just to be taken as our best guess
> and to check whether there was really fire needs additional facts.  

  

I think wehave to distinguish what or how apramanaworks to give us knowledge from whether we will be able to use it correctly.

  

Anumana is a pramana, a means to knowledge. It is not inherently speculative or guessing of some possibility that needs further confirmation with additional facts.

  

Here the point only is:*if we know smoke is on hill***and***we know vyapti that fire 'pervades' smoke*, then anumana tells us the sadhya fire is also there (though it is not revealed by pratyaksha) - no additional facts needed and no guessing. We haveknowledge of theco-existence of fire on the hill.  

  

This is another case where we have to use the example in the intended sense, which is to inform us how anumana works. Other questions like "Can we really know vyapti of fire in smoke?", "Is that vyapti true?", "are we really seeing smoke, or do we need other facts to confirm?" are secondary topics.

  

The point on "anumana is based on gathered facts" is worthy of discussion. I did talk about this in various ways in my earlier posts. For example, if we saw 100 cases of smoke with fire and 0 cases of smoke without fire (provided we are able to do and do "thorough investigation"), then that is a set of 100 data-points (our gathered facts) that only reveal "Where smoke, there also fire". The vyapti is true in that set of 100 data-points. But is vyapti true in general? Technically we have to call it (postulate) the "vyapti-hypothesis" because our data-points establish a correlation only in that set of 100 points, and we don't have a reason to think this correlation is true everywhere we find smoke. But we also don't have a reason to suggest it is false; no evidence to suggest falsity of vyapti. It depends on how our system of knowledge works: does it accept the vyapti-hypothesis so long as it is not contradicted in the perceived world? Science seems to build like this. I don't know how Vedanta approaches this question and it seems Indian schools have different opinions in the matter.

  

The more stringent requirement is to establish some deeper intrinsic connection between sadhya and hetu, example: find causality. We understand in our definition of smoke its causal connection with fire; so vyapti is included in the knowledge of smoke. Thus if we identify smoke correctly, then automatically anumana tells us that fire is there (in some technical sense, not necessarily as burning flames). In our knowledge systems, I think naiyyayikas say that vyapti is known through some alaukika pratyaksha (extraordinary perception); this is like "perceiving" the causal link when we see the data-point for correlation (i.e. when the yogi sees an instance of fire and smoke together, he is also able to see the causality at play and hence he knows vyapti.).

Again, you are not using the examples correctly in my opinion. Statement 2 is not correct usage of anumana unless you accept the vyapti "Whoever is fat must eat lots of food". But clearly you don't accept that vyapti since you point to alternate causes like medical issues; so you should not be calling the logic as anumana.

  

If we want to use the traditional example "Devadatta is fat and not eating in day time. Therefore he must eat at night." for arthapatti, then we have to understand that the example is meant in a casual sense or perhaps they were not that concerned or aware of medical causes for obesity. Or we should simply drop the emphasis on "fat" altogether and replace: "Devadatta is not eating in day time. So, he must be eating during nights."

  

Apart from this I tend to agree with your understanding arthapatti as the endpoint of logical analysis.

  

We have a basic vyapti: "If you are a human, you must eat sometime." 
(Is this regarded a type of vyapti? Assuming so here.)

  

We have an observation: Devadatta is a human.

  

Anumana tells us Devadatta eats sometime.

  

We have a secondary observation: Devadatta does not eat during day-time.

  

The only logical explanation: Devadatta eats during nights.

  

So arthapatti through logical analysis based on our observations and inference leads to a necessary unique conclusion that may itself be beyond pratyaksha (and hence technically beyond direct inference as well). Hence our knowledge via arthapatti is referred toas postulation in English though that should not be taken as arbitrary assumption.
