Namaskaram Prashant-ji,

  

You can compare this with the notion that tarka is not pramana for what is known via shabda but it can be used to support shabda. However we certainly feel often that tarka "proves" our shabda-derived knowledge. Likewise the correlation studies give us a strong sense of acting like pramana, though they are not quite the same.

  

Some example came to my mind:  

  

Suppose someone flipped a coin 100 times and found it landed heads 75 times. What exactly do we "know"?

  

We know that in the observed data, the coin landed heads 75% of the time.

  

This is a "correlation" in our data. Can we know "causality" from this, for instance, does our correlation inform us that the coin is biased? NO. A biased coin would explain the result sensibly; if the coin is unbiased, then the reason for the biased result would be beyond our analysis.

  

But fact is that the correlation in the data does not inform us of causality: it can support that knowledge (like logic can support shabda) but is not by itself the pramana for that knowledge. We cannot do arthapatti here as the only possible explanation, nor do we have alaukika pratyaksha regarding the coin from this data.

  

Without knowing that the bias is intrinsic to the coin, what does our extending the correlation to subsequent coin tosses with that coin mean? We wish to "predict" that if the same coin is tossed 1000 times, that around 750 times Heads will occur. Or claim that the next coin toss has 75% chance of getting Heads because the previous 100 showed 75 Heads. But the prediction is based on a "vyapti-hypothesis" that what we observed in limited data is true in general. Perhaps it can be justified as the most 'sensible' explanation and we take decisions on that basis. But it cannot be asserted as doubt-free knowledge beyond the dataset that we already have observed.

  

Here is a current example. We hear of a company Pfizer say that the covid-19 vaccine works with (say) 90% efficacy. They gave the vaccine or placebo to over 40000 people (assume for simplicity that 20000 got vaccine-candidate and 20000 got placebo). However the actual result goes something like this: Among the first 170 people who got sick with covid, around 90% were the people who got the placebo and only ~10% of the sick patients had received the vaccine-candidate.

  

Do we know the drug actually will work that much better (if at all) for the general population (that the internal factors that distinguish people will have parallel distribution and find parallel response from drug vs placebo)? No. Clearly too much simplification. Our correlation result supports the causality or general correlation hypothesis; and our 'knowledge'-system may allowus to make that hypothesis until subsequent observation contradicts it in a defined manner. But we cannot quite call it Knowledge of drug's efficacy.
