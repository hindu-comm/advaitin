Namaskaram,

  

I am not sure what is the import of this "agreement" or "truce", and am also confident that many of us will remain confused about the reduction to an issue about words and meanings.

  

Let me state the bit of what I follow.

  

Consider the case A =\> B (but not necessarily B =\> A).

  

A = Coin is biased

B = Coin-flip experiment (with that coin) shows ~75% Heads consistently. (If you wish, make it exactly 75%)

  

A = Vaccine effectively kills virus

B = Vaccine trial shows 90% sick patients from placebo group and only 10% from vaccine group. (If you wish, make it 100% to 0%)

  

Can knowledge of B give prama of A?

  

The answer I understand is NO.

  

Science recognizes this fact and hence says that no matter how often, how emphatically and how extensively B is confirmed, there is no way it can "prove" A from that knowledge (either in sense of causality or in sense of general correlation.) For that, we would have to be able to conduct the experiment in all cases, "past, present, future" to assert doubt-free knowledge of its consistency with A. This cannot be done, so A cannot be known in this way: science legitimately discounts its pseudo-pramana for the knowledge of A. However its knowledge-system provisionally accepts A ("as if true") so long as its investigation of B does not contradict it.

  

This is not merely an issue about the limitations in our ability to conduct and analyze the experiments correctly. It is about the prameya of the experiments/methods; the prameya is never A. (In mathematics, I said before, finding new confirmations of a conjecture can never constitute proof which in that knowledge-system is the only way to obtain prama.)

  

The same is true at a more fundamental level in Science's model for perception of the external world. By asserting the perceived world is of a projection in the brain and distinct from the external world, it precludes the possibility that pratyaksha is pramana for its provisionally-postulated external world. We are not told about this bluntly in popular culture, but the philosophers know that the prama in theirsystem is not of the "real world" which is merely a justified-true-belief or dogma.

  

In the Indian tradition, the prameya is A directly. Hence the right application of pramana can give prama of A ("in theory"; but I believe the tradition says "true knowledge" is obtainable). This is like "proof" in mathematics, or checking the weight distribution and material composition of the coin tofind the bias. But can we always be 100% sure? Are there not assumed "proofs" that are later shown to have error? Can not weight/material analysis be done imprecisely due to subtle defects, limitations and obstructions to our instruments and their use? Likewise with perception, even if pratyaksha is held to inform directly of the "real world" as it is, there can always be defects in our sense-organs and other obstructions to the application of pramana, that we are not aware of now and will be discovered later; there is no way to negate all such possibility of doubt even if presently we assume that we have prama of A. That is the thrust of Raghav-ji's argument.

  

So, there are two types of "doubt" here. In the first case, we will always doubt A since our pramana only obtains prama for B. In the second case, even though our pramana obtains prama of A (in theory), we cannot be sure if we applied the pramana correctly - so the prama of A will remain doubt-ridden (for the general person). In both cases, the common person "feels" prama for A and assumes it for their vyavahara; and in both cases, there can arise new discovery that discounts its truth.

  

So the question becomes whether (for the Vedantin) the line of distinction between Science and tradition in their obtaining Knowledge is only of a theoretical interest and can otherwise be safely bypassed.

  

thollmelukaalkizhu
