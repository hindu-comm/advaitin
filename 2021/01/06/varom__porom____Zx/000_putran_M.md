Question:  

>   
> Varom....Porom. \[we come... we go\]
>
>   
>
> Edhukku Varom? \[why do we come?\]
>
>   

  

Answer:

  

If you leave out religion and philosophy, it is just nature at work. You are born because your parents decided to have a child and the laws of biology, physics and chemistry brought about your body in which this miraculous self-conscious I shines. That answers the why.

  

If you are willing to be religious and philosophical, you are here in this body-mind due to your karma in past lives. Your parents are not direct cause but instrumental/secondary; your own inherited conditioning necessitates expression in this new body and in corresponding social and family circumstance. It presents the ideal setting for the jiva toexperience its karma-phala, to work out its adrishta (mental conditioning based in ignorance of Self) by fulfilling svadharma etc., and progress towards moksha. It can also simply screw up the opportunity, accumulate karma-load and adrisha and regress: the cycle simply continues. So you are here to work your way towards moksha, i.e. to remove the fundamental ignorance of Self and realize you never "varom or porom".

  

Q:

  

But why does all this happen? What's the ultimate point?

  

Is there a creator who decides all this?

  

Why not moksha for alland be done with it?

  

Why this varom...porom for the shareeram?

  

A:

  

hmm.. you have to take some of the responsibility then. Why did \[N2\] varom? \[N1\] decided to have a child. Then there is a consequence of \[N1\]'sdesire. Likewise \[N2\] in .. past births had done actions borne out of desire, and there will be consequence. Why should you be given Moksha when you are seeking things in the world? It would not make sense. You get exactly what you deserve.

  

But technically, in advaita perspective, you are already the Self; why don't you stop dividing Self as me vs not-me? It is you who is assuming the body-mind as self and then seeking moksha from the consequence of such identification. Kali has two sides; know Her the Same in both. If you forget Her, then you see the good and bad, the me and you, the life and death as opposites, where one is to be saved or liberated from the other. It is a condition of Ignorance of Self and not of bondage of the Self; but because the mind is entrenched in this ignorance and frames questions from that standpoint, the answers must also pertain to that standpoint. So, to go beyond good and bad, dharma and adharma, life and death, you must choose good, dharma, life; work right; desire right. You cannot act like Arjuna tried; because you (the body-mind identified jiva) have done too much for too long to trap yourself in this state of wrong identification; so start working to cleanse the mind, to focus it on spiritual work, righteous action and knowledge and at the same time confront the apparent dualities thrown at you with the sword of received Knowledge. Don't fear the nonsense of the deluded world; at its level stand up for Dharma and beyond, stand up for the Self that you are.

  

Q:

  

The law of moksha - how did it come about? Who created the rules and the law?  

  

A:

  

A law (if we go along with "law") is not something that has to come about. It is a reading of Reality as it is; and we cannot say that there was a time when it was not.

  

So this situation of a jiva, its state of ignorance of Self and self-identification with bodies and the consequent search for fullness/peace/happiness (aka liberation from suffering/ignorance, or moksha) is said to have no beginning. It is a part of the cycle of manifestationin the existence/Reality that we call Ishvara. In Advaita, this moksha can be attainedin a human body-mind via the transcendent knowledge of Self. The jnani does not ask thesequestions since notions of varom and porom, of bandha and moksha are not applicable to the Self. However from the standpoint of the jiva (ajnani), we may understand that jnana in general also eliminates the causal factor (ignorance of Self) needed to sustain and impel the subtle and causal bodies into further births in gross bodies; so in that sense also, reincarnation ceases and hence the jiva is said to attain moksha from the cycle of birth and death.

As added thought, at some point we start to realize more intensely the impermanence of body-mind, its strength, of our relationships and certainly our possessions; and not take our time here as if indefinite. For the religious, this human birth is an opportunity, said to be hard earned and difficult to attain (durlabha), to progress in the spiritual journey; as that sense grows within, we become more and more careful to use our time for dharma and the 'goal' of moksha; and become diligent in trying to connect with Ishvara: what 'pleases' Him as opposed to what pleases the world? We start to fight (internal) for salvaging every second in a more purposeful way.
