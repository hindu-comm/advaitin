-  The question of the date of Sankara may be taken most correctly as
  that of the 9th century. There has been claims are made in India that
  he lived two thousand years ago, but there is absolutely no proof for
  this claim. The archives at Sringeri Mut have been carefully
  investigated and find that they do not go back farther than the 12th
  century A.D. and that all so-called evidences for Sankara having lived
  two centuries BC are either conjectures or some scholar's
  fabrication.



- Regarding the question of Sankara's death, you may dismiss the legend
  that he did not die, at the age of 32 but disappeared into a cave.
  This is another scholar's story which is quite unfounded. He did
  really pass away peacefully in the Himalayas at that age.

  

- Thirdly it is asked how it was possible for him to have written so
  many books during such a short existence. The truth is that he wrote
  very few books. Those actually written by him were commentaries on
  Brahma Sutras and the Upanishads and on the Gita. All other books
  ascribed to him were not written down by his own hand. They are merely
  second hand collections of notes fraught with transcription errors
  recorded by his disciples from his sayings, talks and discussions.



- Fourthly Sankara's own Guru who was named Govinda lived near Indore.
  When Sankara wrote his commentary on the Mandukya his guru was so
  pleased with it that he took his chela to the Himalayas to visit his
  own Guru named Gaudapada. Only when the latter agreed that the
  commentary was perfect did Govinda release his Chela to start his own
  teaching mission.

  

- Sankara wrote his Mandukya commentary first, then as this revealed
  that he thoroughly understood the subject, his gurus requested him to
  write the commentary on Badarayana's Brahma Sutras, which was a
  popular theological work universally studied throughout India. That is
  why his commentary is written from a lower dualistic point, for those
  who cannot rise higher, save that here and there Sankara occasionally
  has strewn a few pearls, truly Advaitic sentences.



- Biographical anecdotes about his persecution of Jains and Buddhists or
  of his challenges to self-immolation for the loser of a debate, are
  all foolish tales invented after his lifetime either by his own
  followers who took him to be a religious propagator or by his
  opponents like Ramanujists and Dvaitists.



- The religous pundits fascinated by Advaita relate boastfully
  pseudo-historic stories of how Sankara's school put down, persecuted
  end exterminated the Buddhists, as though this was something to be
  proud of. This is because those pundits are mere followers of
  religion, never having understood Advaita Siddhanta. These stories are
  either exaggerations or false fairy tales.

  

- His commentary on Mandukya is pure wisdom, but many of his other books
  are presented from a religious standpoint to help those who cannot
  rise up to such sublimity.

  

- North India is the home of mysticism and deification. South India of
  keen rational truth. Sankara had only four fully trained disciples,
  although he advised some kings. His doctrines spread after his
  lifetime. His books were dictated to secretaries as he traveled. So
  few therefore were capable of understanding his philosophy.

  

- Sankara always traveled, he never lived in a monastery. He simply told
  others "Build a Mut here" and then left because he was busy spreading
  his teachings.



- Sankara says you must first know what is there before you. If you
  cannot know that, what else can you know or understand? If you reject
  the apparent world in your inquiry, how can you cannot get the
  complete realization of truth?

  

- Nearly all Bengalese thinkers hold views of Maya which are entirely
  incorrect and untenable. They do not know Sankara's Upanishad Bashyas,
  but only the Brahma Sutra Bashya. Sankara wrote his Mandukya
  commentary on a beautifully situated island called Omkaresvar, border
  of IndoreState, where Cauvery and Narbadha rivers meet. On this island
  there is also a tomb of Govinda,his guru.

  

- The followers of Sankara nowadays have institutionalized and
  constituted religious sects each contradicting the other. Thus all
  genuine missions ultimately degenerate and finally perish.

  

- Sankara varied his practical advice and doctrinal teaching according
  to the people he was amongst. He never told them to give their
  particular religious or metaphysical beliefs; he only told them to
  give up the worst features of arrogance, aggression and abuse: at the
  same time he showed just one step forward towards the truth.

  

- Sankara was extremely precise and careful in his choice of words. He
  was no lightweight or fool in grammatical writing. Sankara did more
  than write books or initiate missionary Sanyasins: He brought India
  into a unity as a nation. He told men: worship what you wish, remain
  in your particular religion, but remember also you are part of a
  larger whole.

  

- Few learned pundits, scholars and commentators have caught the essence
  of Advaita, they are merely fond of his words. For his spirit is that
  of an appeal primarily to buddhi and reason, with scriptures
  referenced occasionally as a secondary and lesser support.

  

- Sankara enumerated two aspects of reality: the vyavikarika and
  paramarthika satya. He gave religious and ritualistic instructions to
  the populace at large but pure wisdom only to the few who could
  understand and rise to it. Hence the interpretation of his writings by
  commentators is often confusing because they mix up the two
  viewpoints. Thus they may assert that ritualistic practices is a means
  of realizing Brahman, which is absurd.

  

- So many centuries have passed since Sankara appeared; yet it is very
  unlikely to find his true teachings understood anywhere in India
  today. Why? Because so few are able to rise to his level. Hence
  Ramanuja, Madva and other Dvaitists appeared to supply the appeal of
  the common demand.

  

- In summary, Sankara is undoubtedly one of the greatest advocates in
  India's history because (a) he sought to unify the country by placing
  his institutions and teaching from North to South and East to West,
  impressing oneness as a nation on all the people. This oneness is not
  to be restricted by you to religion for it includes its practical
  application to life such as politics and sociology; and (b) he
  accepted and encouraged all the prevailing religions but merely
  purified them of their dross and immoralities. It is false to say he
  attacked or persecuted Buddhism. This was an interpretation by
  pundit-followers who came afterwards and never truly understood his
  message. He has never said that a certain caste or community or that
  Sanyas alone could attain Brahman; he always taught in the spirit of
  universality.
