Namaste,

The problem I think is that the word mithyA is often translated as illusion, which leads to all manner of misunderstandings. In the adhyAsa bhAShya itself, the first two sentences use the word in two meanings.



The first meaning as illusion occurs in the first sentence ending with mithyeti bhavitumyuktam - adhyAsa must necessarily be false / an impossibility, transactionally. The second meaning occurs in the second sentence in the phrase 'mithyAjnAnanimittah' - that the adhyAsa is a result of a mithyA ajnAna. ie sadasat vilakshaNa rUpa ajnAna, an ignorance whose nature is neither sat (absolutely real) nor asat (absolutely unreal).



When talking of jaganmithyA, people misunderstand it to mean that the world is a falsehood, an illusion (the first meaning), whereas what is meant is that the world cannot be said to be real (ie of the nature of absolute reality) nor unreal (ie of the nature of absolute absence), the second meaning.



With the dawn of brahmajnAna, the realisation is that what was taken to be the world is in fact the self, and therefore, no transaction is possible, because there is nothing apart from oneself to transact with. The nivRtti of the world referred to in the talk denotes the understanding that the world as an independent entity has no existence, it is the self that appears as the world. Thus, talk of the destruction of the world after brahmajnAna is misguided.



Until that knowledge arises, transactions (included within which is one's duty, as laid down in the scriptures) will and must be performed. Those actions continue to have transactional validity. In the advaita conception of mithyA, mithyA is vyAvahArika satya and is satya for as long as vyavahAra persists, and therefore, the charge that advaita postulates the falsification of scripture and scriptural duty never applied in the first place, for it to be refuted. The erroneous notion that mithyA meant falsehood should be replaced by the correct notion that mithyA meant vyAvahArika satya, sadasatvilakshaNa rUpa sattA.



That was the import of the Sringeri Jagadguru's talk.
