Namaste Sri Neelakantan-ji,

  

I knew from the beginning that this will be a difficult book to read. The difficulty arises primarily because of two reasons:



i\) The book tries to untangle the considerable amount of confusion that has been created in the post Cartesian period with regard to a host of philosophical concepts. For example, Western philosophy mixes up mind and consciousness and treats the two as if they are the same entity, it treats qualities such as color, sound, etc, as subjective qualities not belonging to objects and thus creates a dichotomy between what it calls 'folk psychology' and 'scientific truth', it has no clear conception of how the meaning of words can be objective entities, it has no clear conception of the distinction between mind-dependence and mind-independence, etc. All these things have been examined in detail from an Indian philosophical perspective in the chapter titled*'Prolegomena to the Direct Theory of Perception*'. This chapter will certainly be difficult to read, especially considering that such an exercise is being undertaken for the first time in the world, at least in a systematic and cogent manner.



ii\) . The book tries to present an overview of the Indian perspective with respect to two things both of which are essential for understanding the contact theory of perception" first, the notion of embodiment and second, the vision that the diverse cause-effect relationships we see in the world are all strung on one Supreme Cause namely Brahman. It was necessary to treat the topic of embodiment because it is the falsity of embodiment, i.e., the truth that the self is never really embodied, that makes it possible for perception to be direct and transparent. And I submit here that if a philosophy does not have embodiment (along with mithya-jnana as its cause) as a theme in the philosophy, it will never be able to explain perception. This is the reason why no Western philosophy has a coherent explanation for perception. I suspect that this chapter will not be easy reading not only for Westerners but also for un-initiated Easterners.



The purpose of writing the book was primarily to establish pratyaksha as a pramana once again in the modern world. If that purpose is achieved, i will be happy even if readability of the book should get sacrificed in the process. :-)

  

Warm regards,
