Namaste Sri Dennis-ji,

  

I can understand the difficulty. We have been schooled into thinking that things like sound and color are subjective sensations produced in our sensorium when signals from some source reach our sense organs through a medium. But this merely a Representational way of thinking. In Vedanta (as well as in Nyaya and European Scholastic philosophy), things like sounds and color, etc., belong to objects; they are attributes that inhere in substances through the dravya-guna (substance-attribute) relationship.

  

Sound is a guna, and as such it must inhere in a substance. The sound of a column of vibrating air is an attribute of the vibrating air just as the sound produced by the string of a musical instrument is an attribute of the vibrating string. We need to consider sound as an objective property belonging to the substance, air, instead of considering the air as merely a medium through which the vibration is propagated to ultimately produce the sound in our brain.

  

So, what happens when a bell rings? The sound made by the bell is an attribute of the (ringing) bell. But the bell also transmits this sound to the air not merely as a vibration that propagates through the air but as sound itself inasmuch as the sounding belongs to the vibrating air as its attribute (just as sound belongs to the vibrating string of a musical instrument). The sound of the vibrating air is an objective property of the vibrating air. So, we have two objects that are making sound: the bell which made the sound originally and the vibrating air which makes sound as it propagates through the air.

  

I had anticipated that readers would face this difficulty, so I had put in the following words while explaining the time-delay between the visual and auditory perceptions of a lightning:

  

"*In using the expression “sound of the vibrating air”we are at once confronted with the issue of incommensurability since, in the paradigm of science, there is no sound in the vibrating air; it is just vibrating air. In the framework of science, the directly perceived qualities, such as color and sound, do not belong to objects; they are subjective qualia. They are produced by the brain as the subjective sensations of seeing color, hearing sound, etc., after the sense organs receive sensory signals from the objects of the external world, transform them into neural signals, and transmit them to the brain through the neural channels of the body. In sharp contrast to this, the Indian logical tradition holds that these qualities – such as color and sound - are objective properties that belong to the objects themselves and that they are perceived when the sense organs, along with the mind, make contact with the objects. The cause of perception is the conjunction of the observer, through the instrumentality of the sense organs and mind, with the objects. The sensory signals from external objects that arrive at the physical sense organs are not considered to be part of the causal chain of the perceptual process. Instead, the observer is held to be an active entity that reaches out to the external objects of the world."  
  
"In the scientific theory of perception, the sound of the vibrating air, being a subjective phenomenal quality, would be perceived only after the sensory signal reaches the ear and gets processed by a chain of processes ending with the brain producing the sensation of hearing sound. In the Indian theory, the sound of the vibrating air is itself perceived; and it is perceived at the location where it is vibrating (provided that it is within the range of audibility)."*
