*But then will not subsequent intervening columns of air between the observer and the last column of air that was the first within range of hearing also vibrate? And then the sense of hearing would reach out to each of those columns, in which case we would then hear multiple similar sounds with some delay based on the number of such imaginary columns there are between the observer and the last column within range of hearing. In which case we would not hear the sound cleanly once from any object that is either within or outside the range of hearing....but that is clearly not the case. When I am in a music concert, I hear one crisp sound of the notes uttered by the musician.*

  

I am not sure why you are considering the propagation of vibrating air as composed of multiple discrete columns of vibrating air. That is not how it is: the intervening air is a continuum. For each note of sound there is a pulse of vibration that propagates through the air. You hear the sound when that pulse of vibration comes within the range of hearing and you continue to hear it until it goes out of the range of hearing. The time for which you hear the sound is not a single instant but a certain fine time duration. In acoustics, the time it takes for the sound to die down is known as 'decay time'. The decay time is normally a fraction of a second (unless the acoustics of the environment are bad). Then there are interference effects due to waves interfering with one another. All these things are the subject matter of acoustics; they are not phenomena that discount the contact theory of perception. If you look at these phenomena as problems, then they are equally applicable to the scientific theory of perception as well. :-)

  

  

*When I am in a music concert, I hear one crisp sound of the notes uttered by the musician.*

  

No, you do hear the sound of each notes for a finite duration of time. If the acoustic engineering of the hall is good, then that finite time will be low. In an anechoic chamber, the fine time will be almost zero. If you are in a room with bad acoustics, the finite time will be rather high and the sound will lose some of its crispness. It is more a matter of room acoustics than a matter that discounts the contact theory of perception.

  

  

*On a windless day, the column of vibrating air would be stationary with regards to the observer.Perhaps you meant to say that the car is moving relative to the column of air and the column of air is vibrating with the Doppler shifted frequency? Ok if you meant to say that.*

  

The column of vibrating air is not composed of a fixed column of air, it is a pulse that is travelling through the air and making different columns of the air vibrate as the pulse goes through it. So, there is relative motion between the column of air and the observer.

  

  

*But then wouldn’t we again have the problem of multiple sounds: not just from multiple columns of vibrating air within range with slight delays, but also a mixing of the sound of the original object with the multiple sounds of each column within range which may result in a noisy distortion of the original sound due to overlap and delays, instead of a clean predictable calculable Doppler shift.*  

  

Yes, in a real life situation this is exactly what happens. In acoustics, they are known as interference effects, standing waves, etc, all of which tend to distort the original sound but in an open air environment things are not as bad as you are making them sound. :-) Despite all these, the sound is normally heard in a discernible manner.

  

  

*VP (last para of pratyaksham) explicitly dismisses the possibility of multiple sounds.*  
  

There are no multiple sounds because there are no multiple discrete columns of vibrating air. The intervening air through which the vibration propagates is a continuum.
