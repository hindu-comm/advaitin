Namaste Chitta-ji,

  

In your response to my question below about a star that is long gone, but an instrument continues to detect light emitted by it from when it was still alive, I was expecting that you might say that the human observer doesn’t see the star but the instrument may continue to see its light for a long time.

  

In some sense this is the reverse of lighting in the flare in outer space in your simultaneity experiment. When the flare is lit up, you are expecting that a human observer will see it instantly before any instrument because light will take time to reach the instrument. Similarly, when the flare dies out also, one would then assume that a human observer should see its going away instantly, before any instrument detects that it’s light is no longer there. The greater the distance of the flare, the instrument may see its light for a longer time even after it dies. The dying/dead star sounds similar to the dying/dead flare case.

  

Not sure if there is a real need to question the science that an instrument can get light from a dead star emitted from when it was still alive, when we don’t seem to have any real problem co-existing with that.

  

With regards to your question about what my stand is, I am first a student of Vedanta. I give benefit of doubt to what I study with shraddha in Vedantic texts. I have been pondering over the VP theory of perception with an open mind for a long time to ensure that my understanding of it doesn’t contradict any yukti or anubhava. Hence my questions about certain observations that seem to cause some trouble to see if you have reconciled them with the VP framework. In order to really convince oneself, anubhava from science, which is a modern pUrvapaksha would need to be explained and reconciled, not just casually dismissed.

  

Another related observation I have been thinking about is gravitational lensing. As you may be aware, if an observer is looking at the sun during a solar eclipse and the sun has in turn just eclipsed mercury, for a while mercury will continue to be seen beside the eclipsed sun even though it is technically behind the sun. This is due to light rays from mercury being bent by the sun due to the bending of space time due to gravity of the sun. This effect has been observed and written about in considerable detail in science. One can observe galaxies that are behind an obstructing galaxy due to bending of light from the obstructed galaxy by the intervening galaxy. Now if light rays coming from mercury are not responsible for my perception, but it is the observer’s vritti that envelops mercury, how would we explain mercury as being seen even though it is behind the sun because the vrtti cannot cross over an obstruction.

  

Yes, I have plans to get to your book. So you don’t have to take the trouble to answer or discuss these if you have already addressed them in the book. I am just placing the observations that I would like to personally understand better in the context of what VP says and these points may also be of interest to others who are following the thread.

  

Regards,

—Satyan

> > > >
> > > > **Review of:**
> > > >

> > > >
> > > > **Natural Realism and Contact Theory of Perception : Indian
> > > > Philosophy’s Challenge to Contemporary Paradigms of Knowledge,
> > > > Chittaranjan Naik, Notion Press, 2019. ISBN
> > > > 978-1-64678-012-9.**
> > > >
> > > > 
> > > >
> > > > \[Note that the conventional view of how we perceive objects is
> > > > what is called the Representational theory of perception. This
> > > > is the belief that light rays from a source hit the object and
> > > > are reflected to the eye of the observer. The light is focused
> > > > by the eye onto the retina, where it triggers responses in
> > > > receptors. Electrical signals are generated, which pass along
> > > > the optic nerve and thence to the visual cortex of the brain.
> > > > Something else then happens to make us somehow reconstruct an
> > > > image of the original object. It is this representation that is
> > > > actually perceived and not the object itself.
> > > >
> > > > 
> > > >
> > > > The view that the author sets out to replace the above is that
> > > > presented by vedAnta paribhAShA of dharmarAja adhvarIndra and
> > > > other Eastern philosophies. Essentially, this says that
> > > > perception involves the Consciousness reflected in the intellect
> > > > going out through the sense organs and ‘becoming one’ with the
> > > > object, thus generating a vRRitti in the mind, which is
> > > > ‘awareness’ of the object. This all happens instantaneously,
> > > > since Consciousness is everywhere. You can read quite a bit of
> > > > it in my attempt to render it comprehensible from Dr.
> > > > Sadananda’s commentary – part 1 begins at
> > > > [http://www.advaita.org.uk/discourses/knowledge/intro1.htm](http://www.advaita.org.uk/discourses/knowledge/intro1.htm).\]
> > > > 
> > > >
> > > > 
> > > >
> > > > This is a remarkable book and one cannot help but have
> > > > admiration for the time and effort that must have gone into its
> > > > writing. Very few people could have written this, requiring as
> > > > it does an in-depth knowledge of both Eastern and Western
> > > > philosophy and many scientific disciplines. It is a fascinating
> > > > subject and the author largely succeeds in convincing the reader
> > > > of its objective.
> > > >
> > > > 
> > > >
> > > > But my conclusion is that the book is not going to appeal to
> > > > many readers. Its overriding purpose is to convince adherents of
> > > > the conventional, common-sense view of the mechanism of
> > > > perception that their understanding is wrong. As such, it is
> > > > written in a very academic style, rigorous and unambiguous, but
> > > > also not easy to read. Indeed, I have to conclude that it is
> > > > only those readers who have some familiarity with all the
> > > > disciplines themselves who will be able to stick with it. The
