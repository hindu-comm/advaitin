KRISHNAYAN by Kaajal Oza Vaidya

(translated into English by Subha Pande)

(A modest review by ProfVK)

In the 7th chapter of Bhagavad Gita the agony of God is expressed in His own words: vedAhaM samatItAni vartamAnAni cArjuna / bhavishhyANi ca bhUtAni mAM to veda na kashcana. Meaning, I know all past, present and future existences; but nobody knows Me yet. But Kaajal Vaidya seems to have known Him, not only as God Supreme but also as Krishna, the human being! This is what this book is about, certainly one of its kind!

Throughout, we are able to feel ‘the agony of God’ in His last moments before He sheds this mortal coil. The book is fiction alright, but it is so realistic that if a scholar tells me that it is all in Vyasa’s Mahabharata, I will believe it. The whole book is like a soliloquy of Krishna; that is why it is rightly called ‘Krishnayan’ in the style of Ramayan. But Ramayan never presents any soliloquy of Rama; and here is a whole book of reminiscences of Krishna by Krishna! Krishna is talking to us like a super-senior citizen who pours out his memories of his old times, good and bad, happiness and unhappiness, rights and wrongs, hits and misses. No major character of His life is left out: Rukmini, Radha, Mothers Gandhari, Kunti, Yasoda and Devaki, ‘sakhI’ Draupadi, brother Balaram, devotee Akrur and many more.. Every author would agree that it is extremely difficult to write a whole book of 200-300 pages which keep on only regurgitating sorrowful and regretful memories almost all of the same kind, of all old times. Some readers may feel it is all a ‘sick portrayal’. It is not so. The author shows how well she has understood the Gita and the Lord and how cleverly she has been able to imagine what might have been going on in the Lord’s human mind in its last moments. Mind wanders from anywhere to anywhere irrespective of time constraint and the book does the same thing. So now let us see some of the contents! It is very difficult to keep the book down, the way Shubha Pande writes it and I am sure the Gujarati original must be of the same kind.

Kaajal Vaidya has absorbed the Gita so well, that she not only quotes from the Gita but also deftly uses the Gita-teaching to make her Krishna-character speak through His own human mind in all this recapitulation. Lest this review itself goes as big as a book I only give some tiny samples each of Krishna’s rambling thoughts about specific characters in Kaajal’s own words put into Krishna’s human mind, at the time when he has been hurt by an arrow and is consciously getting ready to leave this mortal coil. Just a few samples:

Draupadi sat near Krishna’s feet. She realised what had happened the moment she saw His wounded toe. It was clear that not much time was left. If only one could stop the flow of time. ‘I will not let you go. You just can’t go’. Draupadi took Krishna’s hand in hers. ‘Sakhi, Leaving is how every one sees it, where am I going anyway? It is just another journey. Once a person’s time is up, the next journey has to begin. You know very well, Sakhi, even then you ask.’

‘Why has not my time come? Put an end to my time too’.

‘Is that in my control, Sakhi? Every one is bound to wait for their time. I have done the same’.

Another scene: Mata Gandhari’s lustrous pure eyes swam before him. ‘Son, are you in terrible pain?’ Krishna could feel her soft hands stroking his head. ‘Ma! This is a blessing from my mother, a blessing for liberation’. ‘Krishna, why did you accept my curse?’ ‘Ma, who says it is a curse? It is a blessing! Who but you could have wished for my liberation? Ma! I feel that my time on this earth is over. I just need your permission to leave.’ ‘Krishna,’ Gandhari could not control her tears, ‘it will be a sin!’. ‘You yourself have granted Krishna liberation and the liberator can never be called a sinner’. ‘Krishna, please don’t go! How will we all live without you?’.

There are scores of scenes dramatised in this way through this wonderful chronicle of Krishna’s last moments. Let me stop with two more examples:

Sitting under the peepal tree with wounded toe, waiting for his release, Krishna wondered. Why are women so similar to each other? Why do they all feel miserable or angry at the same things? Did these questions mean anything any more? Why did the three most important women in his life feel the same compassion towards him, worry equally for him and love him with the same fierce intensity? These were his lover Radha, his wife Rukmani and sakhi Draupadi. They all seemed to say: ‘You are the one who has blessed us with immensity. You have made us realise our limitless potential, absorbed our scorching brilliance and comforted us with your soothing presence. You have dignified our femininity while giving us love..

Another scene. Jara was the hunter who wrongfully sent the arrow into Krishna’s foot, thinking it was a deer. But when coming near and seeing the tragedy, he could not control himself. He tried to pull the arrow out of Krishna’s foot but the Lord would not allow it. That was the time when a deer was passing by the nearby river but Jara, even though a hunter who is bound to hunt deer for his own food, turned his face away. Krishna asks him why he did not kill that deer. ‘My Lord’ says Jara, ‘at this moment I am only worried about you – nothing else but your well-being. Not about my self, my hunger or my hunting.’ And Kaajal continues:

“Krishna closed his eyes yet again. He had told Arjuna, ‘Only he who is free from doubt and attachment, and resolutely absorbed in me and works selflessly for the benefit of others will attain moksha or liberation. He whose actions are offered to the Lord and whose apprehensions and doubts have vanished because of love may not be a learned person but is certainly a yogi’.”

Krishna’s closed eyes were expressing the excruciating pain inside. Why was it so difficult to let go of one’s mortal body? Must every human go through the same anguish and pain? Was human life nothing more than a journey that began and ended in pain? ‘jAtasya hi dhruvo kRRityuH ..’ Krishna found his own words hollow. Had he succeeded in freeing himself from his own worldly attachments? …. That is how the book draws you into its net, in its early pages.

Finally, let me say this: There are only four kinds of people: those who understand Krishna but not the Gita; those who appreciate the Gita, but not Krishna; those who can rhyme with both Krishna and his Gita; and lastly, those who care neither for Krishna nor for the Gita. My submission is: All those four kinds will love this book of Kajal Ozha Vaidya. And all of them may get wiser about both the Gita and Krishna.

Prof. V. Krishnamurthy
