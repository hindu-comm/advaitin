In my understanding, Advaita does not get stuck in this type of conflict of who is the superior deity, since for the advaitin, Ishvara is not "bound" by a particular name and form. 'He/It' is Consciousness associated with Maya-shakti, that is manifest as and manifests this nama-rupa universe, similar to the Dreamer who projects and perpetuates the dream based on past impressions. The Dreamer in the context of jagat appears as Prakriti or Nature abiding with an optimal Total Order, whereas in the context of jivas having limited knowledge of Totality and ignorant of the higher Self, He apprehends, decides and acts in a direction that appears optimal for that jiva. This capacity to apprehend the present, then choose, direct and manifest the future is the intrinsic Shakti attributed to the Dreamer Consciousness (so long as we speak in terms of the Dreamer-dream duality*)*.  

  

Advaita however does not specially recognize a separate or unique nama-rupa for Ishvara - rather, we have to realize Him behind/in every nama-rupa, and ultimately realize that our self is non-different from His: the Self is non-dual Consciousness that is absolute reality of All. In order to aid in the process of removing the adhyasa that constitutes our ignorance of Self, the devotee is initially suggested to associate Him with a chosen manifestation that best suits the devotee and will aid him in the spiritual direction. That is the devotee's Ishta-devata. It is not that the devata is negated as mere imagination in the universe; it can be relatively real as the devotee himself. But the devotee makes the connection with that devata to the underlying Ishvara tattva, and hence sees the all-pervading Lord/Consciousness through the lens of the Ishta-devata and attributes His omniscience, omnipotence, omnipresence etc. to the Devata. Certain of these attributes may indeed correspond with the Devata, but from the practical standpoint, this is a special superimposition by the devotee that helps attune the devotee's mind to Ishvara tattva. It is in this spirit that the advaitin approaches the supremacist eulogies on particular gods found in the puranas etc. and therefore he is friendly towards different sects who regard their gods as uniquely synonymous with Ishvara.

  

To understand better, consider the dress example. The same man wears tennis shorts when playing tennis and veshti when doing Sandhyavandanam. Certainly we can look at the ‘tennis player’ and extol him as ‘the doer of Sandhyavandanam’; because we realize and are focused on the underlying person who is same no matter the dress. But still, due to our own identifications, we may prefer thinking of the person in a particular dress that is most attractive to our mind and allows us to easily contemplate on him in a general total manner.In practice however, this is difficult for the ignorant jiva to accomplish since it has identified self distinctly with distinct bodies, hence dualistic bhakti is generally suitable for such a jiva. The formal advaitin therefore has a tougher course to walk in the initial stages since he feels compelled to resolve the conflict of the possible suitability of Dvaita bhakti for him with the knowledge of advaita. It may not be natural and he may have to work a lot harder.
