My answer below may not be satisfactory for all (as it does not attempt to produce a direct reference of the type asked for) but it follows thelogic in my mind. 

  

I begin with certain assumptions based on what I have read/understood and for which we can ask for references and quotations (not supplying here):

  

1\. Tradition looks at the Vedas in a holistic integrated way and not as disconnected and unrelated parts. In fact "The Vedas must be learned along with the Angas and Upangas." etc. It teaches of "ultimate" purpose and "ultimate" truth thatunderlies the rest.

  

2\. Tradition teaches that there are 4 purusharthas: artha, kama, dharma, moksha. They are not independent. artha and kama are to be bound by dharma and moksha is the goal. This means that when Veda talks of artha and kama, Tradition does not cancel the larger picture that ties with dharma and back to moksha.

  

3\. The goal of moksha (in advaita Vedanta) is synonymous with 'acquiring' the Knowledge of Brahman, or 'self-realization'.

  

4\. Tradition teaches that the Veda has essentially two parts, the Karma kanda and the jnana kanda. The karma kanda deals with rituals and the jnana kanda is explicitly focused on the Knowledge of Brahman. The karma-kanda for a Vedantin has a higher purpose for the jiva who diligently lives according to its demands: chitta shuddhi and attaining the necessary preliminary qualifications for the path of jnana.

  

5\. Tradition regards the Veda as divine Scripture, the eternal Word that denotes the Knowledge of Ishvara, the seed nama that Ishvara manifests as rupa-"creation".

  

Point of 5is that the traditionalist looks at once at the Veda as being Brahma-svarupam, so essentially every Veda mantra invokes in his mind the right 'meaning', Brahman; unlike for the nastika who considers it a jada poem. In terms of the vyavaharika meaning for the mantras as well, the holistic picture taught by Tradition (for example, points 2 and 4) informs the traditionalist always that this is an intermediary in the process of his realizing the real meaning - i.e. attaining "Knowledge of Brahman".

  

Above can be an inferential process that justifies "The (Vedantic) tradition does exactly this..." Other paragraphs in the essay expand on this.

  

Quote (Original reply sent back)

  

You are missing the point that everything in the world has to do with Brahman, according to the tradition- only in ignorance it is seen as different from Brahman. The traditionalist/astika regards the nastika's "Veda-poem" book as 'Scripture' - the eternal Word corresponding to Brahman's object manifestations, so yes he regards every mantra as signifying Brahman one way or other, hence sacred. That attribution of divinity underlies whatever vyavaharika overlay the traditionalist may otherwise interpret.

  

Why I said "inner meaning" is because not every traditionalist may see it like this since their focus may be at the superficial level. For example the karma-kanda for a Vedantin has a higher purpose for the jiva who diligently lives according to its demands: chitta shuddhi and attaining the necessary preliminary qualifications for the path of jnana. This will not be explicit to many traditionalists who are not spiritually mature and yet have faith in Veda; they may see only different methods for attaining different results in this life etc. But such a laukika interpretation is not wrong either, only even that is based on faith in the divinity of the Veda. Let them build from there and align themselves with the Vedas, eventually the maturity will come. That is the idea.

  

We have to be careful also about who we mean "traditionalist" when we refer to the old days since there were several schools of thought having faith in the Vedas in different ways. The purvamimansakaswere expounding the Vedas in this "literal" karma-kanda sense, and Adi Shankara re-emphasized the jnana-kanda as the real support of the karma-kanda. Etc.
