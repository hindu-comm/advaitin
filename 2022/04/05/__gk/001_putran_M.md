Namaskaram Saptarshi-ji,

  

The essential problem is the non-dependability of 'knowledge' that is regarded as the determination of individual human beings,subject to the colourations of their adhrishta. If instead it is from revealed scripture, we avoid the problem of having to postulate things regarding their trustworthiness, perfection, omniscience, etc. That is the logic and it looks fine to me.

  

Arguably there is an innate conscience in each of us that tells us right from wrong. But our knowledge will be limited and skewed by our own vasanas, adrishtas, biases, self-love, fears and mental confusions. As the acharya says, there is the possibility of ignorance and (self-) deception in our determinations of dharma even in normal samsaric/samanya contexts. In general such people cannot be regarded as pramana for knowing dharma; and certainly not to know even the concept of visesha dharmas that the shastras speak of. I think this is a logical argument that will make sense to those who agree that there is dharma and adharma for a human being; if there is, then it cannot be known dependably from ordinary individuals. Also, the concept of dharma and the scope of its meaning to a person will clearly differ between an astika and a charvaka (who thinks we are nothing more than electrons and protons) - for the charvaka it is something provisional and personal,and otherwise unreal.

  

Then the question is asked whether we can know for certain that a particular human is omniscient and hence can be regarded as pramana of dharma. The acharya says this will amount to an invalid collection of postulations whose basis cannot be pratyaksha - since the phenomenon is not observed elsewhere. This is also logically sound. But we have to keep in mind that the nastika can turn that argument around and say the Veda must be authored because the unauthored phenomenon is not observed elsewhere. I am not saying it is all equivalent but simply that we have to take the logical argument of the acharya in the right spirit. Logic (tarka) can be used to prove or disprove one position or another, by illuminating the knowledge revealed by the mutually accepted pratyaksha-based pramanas; but in other instances its purpose is to buttress our conviction in the knowledge or belief that is based on shabda - scripture, tradition, etc.

  

Regarding omniscience, Chitta-ji has a section early on in his work where he talks of "Scriptures originating from perfected beings". This is his argument there:

  

*Quote*

*Even though perfection removes the ignorance of the (now) perfected being, it does not nullify that part of adrshta, or accumulated past action, known as prarabda karma, which continues to operate in the world. So, irrespective of the perfection attained by a perfected being, the merits and demerits of past action continue to operate in the world as long as the perfected being lives on earth. Hence, the scriptures that issue out of such perfected beings, even though the author may have achieved perfection, cannot be accepted as ab- solutely impeccable and Xawless. And thus is demolished the idea of Xaw- lessness attributed to all the other scripture too that have come from per- fected beings.*

*Unquote*

  

We have to ask for what is the mark by which we may determine a person is omniscient. Some people may forward a criteria like "if he can predict future events correctly, and no prediction has been proven false", then on the basis of such pratyaksha, we can postulate omniscience. But this may not be satisfactory for all, or they may argue that such can be done through yogic powers of special type. In any case, the acharya does not think such can be suitably established, hence we reject the notion of a person omniscient who knows dharma etc.
