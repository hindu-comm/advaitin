Namaskaram Sada-ji, Venkataraghavan-ji,

  

Thanks for the extensive replies. While Subbu-ji's reply clarifies at the abstract Vedanta level, yours (in my mind) brings to light the difficulty of applying Vedanta in "real life" because the central knowledge of Dharma often evades us: we make-ado Ishvara's role to the best of our ability and inability. I also tend to run the risk of losing sight of Vedanta when I analyze examples from the world with the hope of seeing them through the lens of Vedanta.

  

What you are pointing out is not the doership of karma but that karmic-responsibility will vary according to the extent the karma deviates from svadharma. And svadharma determined by past karma can indeed vary from individual to individual in parallel situations.

  

Let me take up the Boss-Employee example, specifically a Military officer and a soldier under his command, both in a foreign mission. The officer is in a position of power.

  

The officer sees a civilian in the streets past curfew and orders the soldier to shoot her down. Let's say the officer's order is in violation of military law and the soldier knows this.

  

The soldier shoots her down.

  

Whose is which karma?

  

A. The soldier alone shot her, so he alone performed that karma and is responsible for that act.

  

This imo is a factual statement but with the caveat "to the extent the decision and action of the soldier deviates from his svadharma." Which of course is more in Ishvara's knowledge than ours, but we are able to approximately assess that the soldier is acting under command and therefore should be deemed less (negatively) responsible than should he be doing the same act wantonly himself.

  

B. The Officer is not responsible for the soldier's shooting act. He is responsible for his karma of ordering the soldier to shoot.

  

This follows from the fact that the shooting act is the soldier's and not the officer's.

However the language is misleading. It should be more like "The karma of the soldier's shooting act is not transferred to the Officer”. See C. below. (I am also talking logically here, not taking recourse to subtler dimensions to Ishvara’s Order if any based on shabda.)

  

But this is not fair at all. The soldier shot the civilian only because of the power and influence of the Officer over him which the Officer used to achieve his own adharmic iccha.

  

Yes. But what if the soldier refused to shoot the civilian, or the Officer left after giving command and the soldier instead of shooting the civilian told her to run away?

  

Should we think the Officer's karma has now changed? Would it be the case that if the soldier followed orders, then the officer's karma is bad; and if the soldier disobeyed, the officer's karma is less bad since the civilian lived on?

  

That is not logical since the Officer's karma is the same in both cases. He had intent, he gave orders and he expected the civilian to be shot dead. And he is responsible for that karma, or to whatever extent it deviated from his svadharma.The Officer should be (theoretically) charged and punished for that crime in the same way whether or not the soldier obeyed orders. And the soldier should be charged in consideration of the fact of his being under the command of the officer and in a karmic-entanglement where obeying the order is his official duty. etc.

  

  

The situation becomes more confused for us because we mix issues based on end-outcomes. You are treated one way for attempting to murder but failing to achieve it and another if the murder is successful - which may be sensible for us given our ignorance of dharma etc. Let Ishvara fill in the balance.)

  

C. The Officer is responsible for instigating the soldier into an adharmic act.

  

So, yes, if the act is deemed adharma, then the Officer bears causal responsibility in directing it and is punishable for that offense - he can be considered the mula-kaarana for the end result. Though the Officer is not the doer of the act, he has a direct causal connection with that specific karma in terms of desiring and instigating it; so he is responsible for his role in the sequence of events. No more, no less. (See this in the light of the logical separation emphasized in B.)

  

This is different from saying the Officer cursed the soldier or the soldier’s mother in an inappropriate manner and the soldier decided to shoot the civilian due to the way he processed the cursing. It wouldn’t be logical to blame the officer for the shooting by the soldier. Blame him all you want for cursing the soldier if that is deemed adharmic. However, blame the soldier for the way he has trained his mind to link cursing with killing, or link back to other influences that trained him thus. The killing-karma is otherwise disconnected from the Officer’s cursing karma; the former is an extension done by the soldier alone.

  

Again “what is svadharma?” can be a grey-box; but it will not be valid should everyone blame their “programmed mind” as defining their svadharma and in whose abidance they commit their adharmic actions. You can be held responsible for programming it thus and those who aided that programming are responsible for their contribution to that sequence (in influencing you from their position of power specifically in the context of the karma in question).

  

  

——————

  

In the case of brahmanas, yes those born in that varna in a traditional setting have knowledge that while in vyavaharika, there is a
pre-programmed svadharma that corresponds to them and by which they should abide. The “influencers” (like family, acharya, tradition etc) that guide him thus are in alignment with Dharma, therefore they are not karmically responsible (in a negative sense) since the process and their roles is in the natural flow with Ishvara’s Order. Whereas the “influencers” who guide him to deviate from his svadharma, they are karmically responsible for their adharma-influencing-karma and the Brahmana who deviates in the end will still have to suffer for his deviation (under such wrong influence) for his karma of having deviated from his svadharma.
