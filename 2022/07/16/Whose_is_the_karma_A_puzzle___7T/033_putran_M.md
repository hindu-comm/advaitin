My understanding/thinking:

  

The jiva had to do the karma,even what is now what you call"prarabdha". Adharma does not naturally happen; it is deviation from dharma. Ishvara does not do it for you; He is not a whimsical maniac. You the jiva have to decide against svadharma and go that route, knowingly or unknowingly. Thereby you accrue more karma that will obtain phala in the future.

  

Simply put, the kriya you do now (and more so, the kriya iccha) is influenced by the past becauseyour decision is based on self-identifications of various kinds. But it is not a direct result of the past; you are still the king on the chariot and in vyavaharika, the director of the charioteer and chariot - albeit the road,the horses, the chariot, the charioteer will influence your choices and have a bearing on the end outcomes. All the other factors, the road you are presently traveling on, the charioteer and horses, all of them can be attributed to your past karma; but in spite of them all, you are still the constantself-determining Consciousness of the future status of all of them. Because of the ajnana factor, your decision will generallynot be perfectly aligned with dharma and you will often tend to take "This is best for me" decisions that are only best for the "wrong" "me" and contrary to dharma.

  

You may not be saying against this; I cannot tell your usage of the word "result".

A is not destined to do what he does. You are imo making the same mistake over and over (I think it is more about language than your intended meaning). You are removing jivatvam from A and making him a jada entity being forced by prarabdha. A made his choices, let it be. What I can agree with - and this is important - is that A cannot do somethingthat results in B experiencing something that is not aligned with his own prarabdha. I can choose to throw a stone at you; that you get hit or not is dependent on your prarabdha. But once our prarabdhastake us to a meeting point and after the I have iccha to throw the stone, at that point I am still the deciding authority to throw or not throw the stone. The choice is mine, not prarabdha's orIshvara's.

  

It is the game of Ajnana to make the self imagine it is bound by causality.

Aside from that, I think we are coming to the central pointsbehind my questioning.

  

Here is how I understand this:

  

B's experience of jnana of A's actions is **entirely** aligned to his own past karma (hence his responsibility, not A's) and fits him presently as karmaphala. (A chose a direction available to him due to his prarabdha but from the standpoint of B, A's action is catalyst for B experiencing his karmaphala - in the form of jnana of A's action.)

  

That the jnana of A's action generates in B the iccha for a particular kriya is also **entirely** B's responsibility. Because the status of his subtle-bodyis due to his past karma, how it reacts to the jnana of A's action is his responsibility and apart from its reaction, how he presently directs it further is also his own present doing. So whether of past or present, the transition from jnana to iccha is entirely B's responsibility. A is outside of the picture.

  

Finally, the new kriya, to whatever extent it deviates from svadharma, is B's responsibility. To the extent the kriya aligns with dharma, it becomes superfluous to assign responsibility to B. The ultimate judge of dharma-abidance is Ishvara.

  

But however that be, B's kriya is not A's responsibility whether A wanted it to happen or not. A is entirely responsible for its jnana, iccha, kriya including for its iccha/kriya ofinstigating B towards its kriya. But that B obtains jnana of A's action, gets instigated in a certain way and then does some action in response is entirely B's responsibility.

**Let's look at the Husband-Wife-Cat example.** The end result is that the cat is hurt.

  

Vedantin: The result experienced by the is as per prarabdha.

  

The Husband points to his body-mind condition and his wife shouting at him.

  

Vedantin: Wife is at fault potentially for shouting at the husband and any other adharmic iccha in her mind that prompted her kriya. She is not at fault for the Husband's own iccha and kriya. The Husband is at 'fault' for his experience of wife-shouting and getting disturbed thereby, the body-mind condition that gets thus disturbed, and the new kriya of kicking the cat. How much the new kriya is a deviation from dharma will depend on the extent to which the other factors impel the decision.

  

Now here is the tough question. What if the wife knew of the husband's mind-condition and knew of the possibility that he may "lose it"?

  

That means when she chooses to shout at him, she has jnana that her shouting can instigate him into violence. Such an outcome may or may not be her iccha; but she has jnana of its higher probability.

  

We may expect that her dharma will vary according to this jnana; hersvadharma may be therefore to control herself from shouting at him carelessly. Her deviation from dharma in such a situation may likely be higher than in a situation where there is no such jnana of the fragile husband's mind etc.

  

This is not the same as saying that should she shout, then she becomes responsible for what takes place through her husband. Her dharma is based on *her* action, iccha, jnana; and not based on the husband's further reaction and the cat's suffering. Her husband could be snoring happily when she shouted from the next room hoping the cat is kicked out. Doesn't mean her karma has changed. She should be faulted for it independently and without mixing it with the husband's own breach of dharma or the final outcomes. His past and present karma come to the fore in *his* reaction; and if that is deemed adharma, then he has to be duly faulted for his iccha and kriya. (faulted means "will obtain suitable negative karmaphala.")

  

The point is that we cannot transfer the responsibility of the husband's poor mind onto the wife; it stays with him. He built it through his past karma. That she sought to abuse his mind is her karma; and if she sought to achieve a deviant end through such abuse, then it is adharma of an even higher nature. BUT so long as the husband is a necessary jiva intermediary between her action and his kriya, his deviation from dharma must be separated from hers. She is not causally responsible for kicking or not kicking the cat but for shouting and possibly seeking to instigate the kicking etc.

  

And if she did not even seek to instigate but shouted nevertheless in a fit of anger,with same result for the cat, then of course her own deviation from dharma is far less. It would be even more absurd to fault her then for the disturbance the husband suffers from his self-damaged mind and then for his kicking of the cat.

Vedanta should be seen as the crest jewel of Sanatana Dharma/ Hinduism. And sanatana dharma is not something separate from "day to day" events. I am not asking for a true-false answers in every context; as we say, even dharma is beyond our knowledge in general. But we should not shut out explorations of the dimensions of criticalthinking found in our religion and how they apply to the questions posed by the world, and the possibility of confronting poisonous arguments and philosophies inimical to the Dharma. For example, if we say anumana is a pramana, then the Vedantin should be able to discern "inferential" arguments in the world with reference to day-to-day events that are not conforming to his rules, and take up a strong position against them and present his own viewpoints on them. Likewise as in the problem we are looking at presently, the world takes decisions based on its judgments on causality and dharma; those decisions affect us. If Vedanta has no say, then fine; we go with the purvapakshior nastika logic in those areas. But that "if" has to be explored carefully; and if we find that Vedanta does have its own take, then so it is, that is our position and way of looking at it. Such confrontations can also finetunes us in our own religion and foundations of tradition.
