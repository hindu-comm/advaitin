Let us take up this problem in a simplistic direct way, and ask how do we renounce a particular habit,call it A.

  

I say I want to stop doing A.

  

I have to ask and answer for myself a few basic questions:

  

1\. Why?

  

2\. What will I gain by stopping A? (necessary consequence of stop-A/not-A)

  

3\. What can I gain by stopping A?

  

4\. Can I afford not gaining that? Do I really want or need that?

  

4\. What will/can I lose by not stopping A? Can I afford losing that? Is it really needed?

  

5\. a) Is A essential to my existence, identity, being?

  

  b) Can I let go of association with A and still be Me, grounded and stable? What am I and what/how would I be without A? What will/can happen to me or my life if I stop A?

  

  c) Is my bondage to A an intrinsic real part of Me? Or is it an unreal superimposition by me that really has nothing to do with Me?

  

-------------------

  

Ok. The Mind typically spits out answers in both directions. It vacillates. It lacks clarity and we see in/through it competing validities of A and not-A.

  

Krishna says\* abstinence first and realisation of Self finally. But even to have secure abstinence, the mind must have (even if be goaded, tricked, or imposed into) requisite*clarity*that not-A alone is valid and viable and*A is simply not an option.*

*  *

"The objects recede from an abstinent man, with the exception of the taste (for them). Even the taste of this person falls away after realization the Absolute." The simplest method for striving for this is to concretize/represent not-A as B, the positive entity or Goal, that stands diametrically opposite to A, in whose presence the bondage to A loses reality. B is the thing you want to gain and cannot afford to lose. If you want a happy work environment, you stay disciplined and wake up on time everyday. The mind is clear, of cause and effect, here. If everytime the mind turns to a habit (simple case here: that it regards non-essential otherwise, and generally can be identified in an isolated manner), the bhakta brings in his B: the Lord's name-form, and force turns the mind to the Goal of seeing Him, the mind learns after a few uncompromising reprimands that A is not an option for B will inevitably come in between.

Quote

  

Suppose I have a habit of drinking coffee.

  

Can we say it is my dharma to drink the coffee?

  

Yes in the following sense. The Self identified with this body-mind is inclined to follow through on the vasanas of that identified state, and hence to seek and drink coffee. When the Self is identified with the "addicted-body", the karma of satisfying the addiction is within the bounds of dharma in the context of that identification.

  

If we ask again, how can we consider it "dharma", even when such an action is based on a negative thing like an addiction?

  

The answer is: because the body-mind-world is a jada projection of the all-pervading antaryami Ishvara, any and every possible direction of evolution by Ishvara of this BMW is within the realm of Dharma or His Order.

  

In that case, how can we differentiate dharma and adharma in the relative plane?

  

Thekarma done is dharmic with reference to the karmaphala that it obtains; paradharmic with reference to goals/results it is not connected to; and adharmic with reference to goals that it directly prevents or obstructs from obtaining. So, drinking coffee is dharmic when the objective is a caffeine boost for that habituated body-mind; it is paradharmic to cure a bacterial infection; and it is adharmic (for some) for the purpose of getting a quick nap right after (if that caffeine boost conflicts with one's ability to sleep).

  

Now let us consider two types of self-identifications.

  

1\. The physical level, the Self identified with the human body, seeking its security and preservation, its sensual fulfillment and success in a temporal life as a samsarin.

  

2\. The subtle level, the Self identified as the jivatma, seeking moksha.

  

Suppose a person is born in an orthodox brahmana family but leaves all that vaidika svadharma to "follow his passion" and become a businessman. Is he necessarily doing something adharmic? No, he cannot but follow a viable path in Ishvara's Order which is dharmic with reference to the corresponding karmaphalathat can follow. He aligns to the Dharma or Order of a part of Ishvara's manifestation and furthers its evolution in accordance with that self-identification.

  

The question really is whether he has knowledge of this manifestation as it wholly is, of its dharma, and of the karmaphala that will obtain from his karma. To the extent that is true, he is abiding by the dharma of his self-identifications in Ishvara's manifestation. Typically however,

  

A. There is variance between what he thinks is Ishvara'smanifestation and Order and what it really is, and hence his actions can veer towards adharma with respect to that manifestation thatthe self is identified with. An extreme example is where a man hallucinates his body to be a buffalo's and therefore starts to eat grass. Eating grass is adharmic for his body though it is dharmic for his hallucinating mind.

  

B. Or, he is aware of and participates in different levels of self-identification with Ishvara, and hence engages in karma that is dharmic at one level and adharmic in another. A simple example for this is when a diabetic eats sugar. He knows it is adharma for his body as a whole but indulges in it anyway for the dharmic-sake of the 'tongue' that finds the desired satisfaction.

  

The physical or gross level of self-identification is only one dimension of Ishvara's manifestation. At the subtle level, the Self is identified (or manifests) as the jivatma seeking moksha. In this context, the Brahmana forsaking his varna dharma and becoming (for all practical fact) the vaishya deviates from svadharma since such karma will impede the attaining of chitta-shuddhi, jnana, moksha. That is, that jivatma is not aligning to the Dharma of its present manifestation, with reference to the goal of Moksha from samsara. ("I am a Brahmana" isrelevant only with reference to the subtle level of manifestation as jivatma.)

  

Now, let us return to coffee.We already explained that there is nothing intrinsically adharmic about the habit which is a part of Ishvara'sOrder. My drinking of coffee is dharma in the context of self-identification with the habituated body-mind.However, if we should attain shraddha and clarity in a competing self-identification within Ishvara in which context the habit of drinking coffee is adharma (or at least paradharma), then perhaps we would have the necessary impetus to do sadhana for overcoming thehabit.. Perhaps the habit can be separated as a superimposition on an essentially "free-of-coffee" body-mind that is one's "reality"; that therefore the habit is removable, i.e. that there is a dharmic path in Ishvara'sOrder leading to its removal.

  

In the extreme situation where one is unable to discern this line of separation and the habit/addiction seems inalienable from all levels of body-mind identifications, and yet one is desperate to overcome its hold, then the final recourse is (fight from the standpoint of) the jnana of the Self that is not the body or the mind. Not that it will resolve quickly the manifest 'problem', but that the ground of Jnana burns out all reality out of the body-mind-world adhyasa on Self and this includes the attached coffee habit.
