One can consider a vairagya-based vratha in 3 ways.

  

1\. Abstinence. Simply cut off from indulgence in the desire altogether. This can have serious withdrawal symptoms initially but if we can get past that preliminary phase, then it is easier to maintain so long as we can take a basic amount of precaution to avoid the lures. Initially, the fall is only a step away - but we keep that step away diligently. Once we re-program our routines and the mind's expectations, we may find it possible to go in and come out, every now and then, like Hanuman without getting inextricably caught in the desire-cycles.

  

2\. Compartmentalization. Allow the mind to indulge in its dvaita worlds happily during the fixed period in the vratha. But once outside those times, re-commit to the standpoint of knowledge where the same objects are now looked upon as manifestations of Ishvara or our Ishta-devata. Keep training the mind so it gets used to this process of sublation of its desire-based dualities in Ishvara, bhakti, jnana. But this is done with disciplined adherence to the vratha times: that 1 hour of cricket, we watch like any other fan; but once the hour is up, we withdraw and demand the mind to lift to the higher standpoint and practice vairagya from there.

  

(The mind can of course play a host of tricks in this approach, especially in how it goads us to tweak and cheat around the corners. We have to do our bit of back-and-forth fighting to improve the vratha rules etc. so we can optimize its effectiveness. If such doesn't seem to work, we may want to go into the abstinence mode for a certain amount of time in order to strengthen our confidence and detachment and clear our heads.)

  

3\. Advaita at all times. This is more of a process towards a goal. As we work on 1 and 2, we may become more rooted in the standpoint of knowledge and bhakti. Then when we do face the objects of desire, with the option to indulge them, we may naturally test ourselves to practice advaitic or bhakti thinking and detachment even while the body-mind experiences them. This would not be a formal part of sadhana or vratha but something naturally incorporated as we develop in training the mind and more formal sadhana.

  

This sort of vratha, even though focused on some particular desires, can be seen as representing the general advaitin figuring out samsara. Very few go into formal sannyasa, so we have to figure out how to contemplate advaita even as we deal with our vyavahara. This is the normal challenge for us but a formal vratha highlights the obstacles and practicing there will help us apply more generally.
