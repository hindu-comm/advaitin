As far as I understand, yes there is validity for rope-snake analogy where snake represents the jagat seen in ignorance (of having existence different or independent of Brahman) and which goes away in knowledge.

  

The duality that remains in the cognition of a jnani but which he knows to be nothing independent (only appearance/denotation) of Brahman, that is mithya. It is there in our relative standpoint but not there in the absolute standpoint of Brahman. For an analogy, there is appearance of water vs chair in our relative standpoint - even if we know (as per physics) that both are fundamentally only manifestation of energy; but from the standpoint of energy itself, such duality is non-existent since there is only itself - beyond words and descriptions.

  

1\. Ignorance: There is water, there is chair. Both are \[fundamentally\] different.

2\. Knowledge: There is only Energy that appears in different nama-rupa, call it water or chair.

3\. Energy alone is - beyond words and thought. Absolute non-duality.

  

Of course, this is for analogy since brahman should not be equated with energy; but there is a useful parallel.

  

The jnani knows standpoint 3 but he himself functions in standpoint2 (based on seer-seeing-seen division) where the dualistic appearance is also a fact. Standpoint 1 is the snake that vanishes in the knowledge of standpoint 2; but standpoint 2 can also be referred to as snake relative to standpoint 3 beyond the seer-seeing-seen. I am saying it because there may be places where such usage comes up. Typically however the usage makes best sense when we talk of standpoint 1 vs 2 where jnana removes the snake and the jnani realizes that there is only Brahman (appearing as if dual).

Another approach:

  

When the person sees the rope and thinks it is snake, he is actually seeing the rope (via eyes) + the added thought-objectification (in his mind) that it is a snake. This snake of his imagination is superimposed onto the rope. It is false knowledge because its meaning content has no real existence. It seemingly points to something (snake) but that something has no existence in the rope. That something is therefore a vivarta ‘transformation’ (imagination, appearance) of the rope in the mind of this ignorant person. It is mithya, not unreal because it has an apparent existence in the world of imagination (i.e. it exists as a mind-object that survives so long as there is adhyasa) and not real because it is destroyed in knowledge of rope.

  

Right knowledge does not necessarily eliminate variant appearances of the rope (in dull light, coloured light etc.); however in all such appearance, the object seen is known to be only rope - the original notional snake is destroyed.

  

When it comes to Brahman, though there is nothing other than or apart from Brahman, we imagine a world of duality and jada existence different from Brahman. The imagination itself is a mind-object that has existence as thoughts in our mind. But the content of the imagination posits non-existence (jada world) as existence (Brahman). Thus this object (thought) points to knowledge of itself that contradicts itself (i.e. its own reality). It is a self-contradiction (gibberish) like a barren woman’s son. The phrase exists as a word-construct (‘projection’ of words), it appears to mean something, but that something is totally belied in right knowledge. Likewise, the world of imagination vanishes in the knowledge of Brahman, and whatever duality remains is only the appearance/'projection' of Brahman.
