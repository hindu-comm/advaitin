Q: Did Shankara believe that 'darkness' was a physical entity and, if so, how did he rationalize this?



A: Yes, Adi Shankaracharya did consider 'darkness' (तमस् or tamas) to be a physical entity, but this concept needs to be understood within the broader metaphysical framework of Advaita Vedanta.



**Shankara's View on 'Darkness'**



In Advaita Vedanta, as articulated by Shankara, 'darkness' is often associated with avidya (ignorance) or maya (illusion). While 'darkness' in the physical sense refers to the absence of light, in a metaphysical sense, it symbolizes ignorance, which obscures the true nature of the Self (Atman).



However, Shankara did sometimes treat darkness as a physical entity that has tangible effects. For instance, in his commentary on the Brahma Sutras (particularly 2.2.28), Shankara addresses the debate about whether darkness is a substance (dravya) or merely the absence of light. He leans toward the view that darkness can be treated as a substance because it appears to have tangible effects: it can be seen (or rather, experienced), it can obscure vision, and it can be perceived as something distinct from light.

****

**Rationalization**



Shankara's rationalization of darkness as a physical entity comes from the idea that it possesses characteristics that make it more than just a mere absence. Here’s how he might have rationalized this:



- Perceptibility: Darkness is perceptible to the senses. Even though it
  is experienced in the absence of light, it is still something that is
  noticed by an observer. This perceptibility suggests that it has a
  kind of existence, even if it is not an independent entity like light.



- Effect on Objects: Darkness can obscure objects, preventing them from
  being seen. This obscuring effect is treated as a quality that
  darkness possesses, much like how a physical object can block light or
  cast a shadow.



- Comparison with Ignorance: Just as darkness covers or conceals
  objects, ignorance (avidya) covers or conceals the true nature of
  reality. In this analogy, both darkness and ignorance are considered
  to have a kind of substance or reality in that they have the power to
  obscure. However, just as darkness is dispelled by light, ignorance is
  dispelled by knowledge (jnana).



- Vedantic Metaphysics: In the non-dual framework of Advaita Vedanta,
  everything that appears to exist within the realm of duality,
  including darkness, is ultimately a manifestation of maya. Maya itself
  is neither fully real nor fully unreal; it is the power that creates
  the illusion of duality. Within this framework, darkness can be seen
  as a real experience within the illusory world, even though it is
  ultimately part of the same illusion that veils the non-dual reality.



**Conclusion**



Shankara's treatment of darkness as a physical entity is rooted in his metaphysical understanding of how the world operates within the illusion of duality. By treating darkness as a substance, Shankara aligns the concept with the broader idea of avidya—something that has real effects in the world of appearances, yet is ultimately unreal when viewed from the perspective of non-dual Brahman.



This perspective reflects the intricate way in which Advaita Vedanta navigates the relationship between the phenomenal world (which includes experiences like darkness) and the ultimate, non-dual reality.

A: Yes, Adi Shankaracharya does comment on Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 3.7.13 in his commentary on the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad. The verse you're referring to discusses the concept of the Antaryamin (the inner controller) who dwells within various elements and entities of the universe, including water, fire, air, space, and so on.

Key Points in Shankara's Commentary



Shankara’s commentary elaborates on the nature of this inner controller as Brahman, who is present within all aspects of existence. In the passage you provided, Shankara is interpreting the verse as part of his broader discussion on the nature of Brahman as the underlying reality within all things.



- Adhidaivata (Cosmic Level): Shankara explains that the inner
  controller (Antaryamin) resides within all the deities or divine
  forces (देवतासु). These include water (apsu), fire (agnau), the
  atmosphere (antarikshe), air (vayau), the heavens (divi), the sun
  (aditye), the directions (dikshu), the moon and stars (chandratārake),
  and space (ākāśe).



- Tamas (Darkness) as an Avrana (Covering): Interestingly, Shankara also
  includes tamas (darkness) in his commentary, referring to it as an
  āvaraṇa-ātmaka (something that has the nature of covering or
  concealing). This aligns with his broader metaphysical view where
  darkness is seen as a substance or entity that can obscure, just like
  ignorance obscures the knowledge of the Self.



- Adhibhuta (Physical Level): Shankara also discusses how the same
  Antaryamin (inner controller) is present within the physical elements
  and beings (भूतेषु) ranging from the highest, like Brahma, to the lowest,
  like a blade of grass (stamba). This shows the non-dualistic view
  where Brahman pervades everything.



**Conclusion**



So, to answer your question directly: Yes, Shankara comments upon Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 3.7.13 and elaborates on the idea of Brahman as the inner controller present in all things, including tamas (darkness). This reflects his broader Advaitic philosophy, where Brahman is the immanent and transcendent reality behind all aspects of the universe, including the dualistic phenomena like light and darkness.



Q: Does anything said by Shankara mean that he considered 'darkness' as literally a physical entity rather than a metaphorical, metaphysical one?



A: No, Shankara did not consider 'darkness' (तमस्, tamas) as a literal physical entity in the sense of a material substance like earth or water. Instead, he treated darkness primarily as a metaphorical or metaphysical concept, particularly in the context of Advaita Vedanta, where it symbolizes ignorance (avidya) or the obscuration of true knowledge.



**Understanding Darkness in Shankara's Commentaries**



- Metaphysical Interpretation: In Shankara's philosophy, 'darkness' is
  closely associated with avidya, the ignorance that conceals the true
  nature of the Self (Atman) and the ultimate reality (Brahman). This
  'darkness' is not a physical entity, but rather a metaphysical
  condition that causes the perception of duality and the experience of
  the phenomenal world as separate from Brahman.



- Figurative Usage: When Shankara discusses 'darkness' in his
  commentaries, he typically uses it in a figurative sense to describe
  the state of ignorance. For example, in his commentary on the
  Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, when he refers to tamas as āvaraṇa-ātmaka
  (having the nature of covering or concealing), he is describing its
  function as a veil over true knowledge, not as a physical substance.



- Philosophical Context: In Advaita Vedanta, phenomena like darkness and
  light are often used to explain the relationship between ignorance and
  knowledge. Light represents knowledge that reveals the true nature of
  reality, while darkness represents the ignorance that obscures it.
  This is a metaphorical framework rather than a statement about
  physical reality.



**Clarification on Brahma Sutra Commentary**



In his commentary on the Brahma Sutras (2.2.28), where Shankara engages with the idea of darkness, he entertains the notion of darkness having a sort of existence because it can be perceived and has an effect (e.g., obscuring vision). However, this discussion is more about the epistemological status of darkness—how it can be known and its effects—rather than asserting that darkness is a physical substance.



**Conclusion**



Śaṅkara did not treat ‘darkness’ as a literal physical entity. His discussions of darkness were primarily metaphorical and metaphysical, focused on its role as a symbol of ignorance in the non-dualistic framework of Advaita Vedanta. Darkness, in this context, is a useful metaphor for explaining how ignorance obscures the true nature of the Self, but it is not considered a physical substance in the material sense.
