Dear Sudhanshu-ji, Jaishankar-ji, Raghav-ji and Akilesh-ji,



You have made some fair points but not altered my position. I maintain that those aspects of Advaita that are likely to persuade a seeker to Self-realization can be explained to any (sādhana catuṣṭaya sampatti qualified) intelligent seeker. I believe it is really only the post-Śaṅkara authors who started logic-nitpicking over everything that was said and complicated the issues. (I.e. I agree with SSS here, although not necessarily with all of his other beliefs.)



I am not prepared to embark upon any discussions of the sort that occupied post-Shankarans but, as regards Sudhanshu-ji’s point I would have thought that all Śaṅkara is saying in Br.U.B. 1.2.1 is that darkness is the word we use to refer to an absence of light. Since we all know what we mean when we speak of ‘darkness’, in that sense it can be considered to ‘exist’. This way of looking at it also answers Jaishankar-ji’s point about ‘presence’ and ‘absence’ – one certainly speaks of the ‘presence of darkness’ and no one is any doubt as to what is meant. One might even say that there is darkness in the cave and others would know what was meant, even if they did think it was a strange way of putting it! But I suggest that everyone would understand it to mean that there was an absence of light (excepting a few Nyāya-influenced Advaitins of course).



I’ve been trying to think of another example. One that occurs to me is the use of the word ‘famine’ in a country where there is no food. Food is obviously something we can hold, and even eat. Famine is a state whose consequences are evident, but it is not something that we can pick up and hold. But I agree that we would have to say that famine ‘exists’ in that country. Nevertheless, it exists because there is an absence of food! It is the way that we use language. Similarly, darkness exists because there is an absence of light. Just as it is food that it the actual ‘thing’ that we want in a state of famine, so it is with light and darkness.



Of course, waking and dream worlds are ultimately equivalent in being mithyā. But no one (including you!) would deny that they are practically different in our experience. This is incontrovertible and presumably why Śaṅkara differentiated them as vyavahāra and pratibhāsa and, as an interim explanation, stated that Īśvara was the ‘creator’ of the former. As I emphasized in my first post (?), Advaita is intended to take us from our initial belief in a dualistic universe to an intellectual realization of non-duality. There is no fruitful purpose in going down academic blind alleys.



I’m afraid you lose me, Sudhanshu-ji, in your last few paras, talking of unreal things being the cause of other unreal things. Apologies for my initial confusion over the word ‘triguṇātmaka’. I have not encountered this word before and was ‘brought up’ in a school that conflated Sāṅkhya and Advaita and used the Sāṅkhyan (real) version of the guṇa-s! I very rarely refer to them at all.



I didn’t think I was being sarcastic on the topic of darkness and dark matter. I certainly do not claim to be certain on any such topic. Who knows what the scientists are going to come up with next? (There are even one or two who seem to have the strange idea that consciousness is fundamental to the universe.) But, as regards space being ‘accommodating’, doesn’t this mean that space is that which CAN accommodate, rather than being that which must already be accommodating something?



I certainly do not deny that advaita requires “slow and careful unfoldment from a traditional teacher” – I have said this many times. What I am saying is that this does not mean it is necessary to invoke obscure Nyāya syllogisms in order to make sense of it or to reinterpret everything that Śaṅkara said in order to make it ‘clearer’ what he ‘actually meant’. And I certainly hope that no one is referring to my ‘Confusions in Advaita Vedanta’ as “paperback Vedānta”! (A third of the publishing run was in hardback.)



Best wishes,

Dennis
