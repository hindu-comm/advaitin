Namaste Putran ji,

  

Pardon me for not being active in the group. I do see the mails and follow sometimes but I get lost in a variety of activities.

This question about gods has to be explained to our youngsters. They do have genuine doubts which decide their attitude to Hinduism, or religion in general.



The third question about millions of gods may be taken up first. I normally start explaining the Upanishadic concept of Supreme Reality, which is satyam jnanam anantam brahma, translating it as infinitely existing consciousness. It is an impersonal entity, denoted by the pronoun 'It'. This consciousness has a manifesting power (maya) and it manifests as the entire creation - cetana and acetana. The first manifestation is Hiranyagarbha, which is described as sarva-devatmaka. It is also called Iswara. What all we call as Brahmaa, Vishnu and Rudra are three cosmic functions in this Hiranyagarbha. All other deities such as Indra, Varuna, Vayu, Agni and others are the universal energies which are responsible for creation from various angles. In this context we can also refer to the Sakalya Brahmanam of the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, in which the 33 koti gods are mentioned. The word koti does not refer to number but it refers to category, as in jantu-koti, prani-koti, jiva-koti etc, where it means a category. The 33 categories mentioned there are the eight vasus (the universal forces which nourish us), the eleven rudras (which make us cry, the five sense organs, the five organs of action and the mind which constantly pester us to pursue what they desire), the twelve adityas (which I understand as forces which guide our intelligence), Brihaspati and Indra. This completes the number 33. Yagnavalkya explains how one Reality manifests in all these forms. We may also refer to vibhuti yoga, while explaining to students. We may also refer to the adhishthana devatas for the indriyas, which are universal forces. In your note you have explained how the individual self cannot be separated from the causal self. The idea of total gross body, the virat purusha, is also explained by you.

  

The second question is whether the gods are imaginary or real persons. I may look like an agnostic but I explain saying that Indra loka, Chandra loka and such are not physical three-dimensional places which exist somewhere in the cosmos or beyond cosmos in the vast expanse of consciousness, but they are experiential states visualized as lokas. Sri Shankaracharya almost says this when he writesते लोकाः कर्मफलानि, लोक्यन्ते दृश्यन्ते भुज्यन्ते इति, जन्मानि, (those states of existence are the result of our actions; those which are perceived, experienced, which mean births). If we see the first few chapters of Sabha parva we find Narada explaining different lokas and the type of people who dwell in them. It is quite logical that a person who has lived the life of an ascetic, meditating on the Supreme will attain tapo loka or Brahma-loka (not Supreme Brahman). Someone who has been a good ruler will enjoy a high status in Yama loka. The subtle body creates its own loka and dwells in it. The description there is quite interesting. We can explain the lokas and gods in this way.

  

The remaining question is whether the gods are moral? This is in the realm of puranas/itihasas which cannot be taken literally. For instance, Krishna appears to tell Arjuna to cut corners; Rama appears unjust when he shoots Vali. You have discussed the issue of morality in your note. Krishna or Rama have a larger perspective of dharma, think of larger good for the world and do not have a limited perspective. It is left to us whether we have to see such incidents from our limited view or otherwise.
