In my essay, I had opined ("to be verified") that the gods are jiva-occupants of certain functional positions 'representing' Ishvara (i.e. Ishvara identified/conditioned to certain functional upadhis in the subtle plane, but fundamentally jivas). If these individual minds are those of "jivas", thatwould mean that post their tenure (which may extend for a full 'cycle of creation'), the (presently) god will have to eventually re-take manushya janma as part of its quest for jnana/moksha and some other jiva will occupy that position. In this case, the subtle-bodies of transmigrating jivas and gods are fundamentally non-different except the gods have acquired certain special upadhis for thepurpose of their temporary stay as heavenly powers for this cycle of creation.

  

I could not get satisfactory confirmation (or negation) for this viewpoint as of yet although the internet generally seems to endorse a more standard alternate perspective ~ that the gods at least ones like Agni are eternal beings, manifested at the start of each 'cycle of creation' and they remain immortal in that cycle; *and* their subtle-bodies cannot otherwise be said to be transmigratory as a jiva (in say a different cycle). In this viewpoint, the subtle-bodies of gods and jivas are fundamentally different even though they share features such as the potential to manifest ego-consciousness.

  

Again, what is this difference? Prior to creation, the jivas of a previous cycle are "remembered" in the causal Maya of Ishvara. In the "god is jiva" view, one such jiva "is born" as god Varuna in this creation and He occupies that position till at least the end of this cycle (barring exceptions due to the Leela-of-Lord). Meaning, that jiva due to its past karma is now endowed with special attributes of Varuna due to which He governs the manifestation of Water in the universe. At the end of this cycle, the gross merges in subtle and the subtle in Causal. The jiva that was Varuna has experienced its earned karmaphala but its essential ajnana and other karma-baggage from past human births remain; and that essential mind is re-projected in the next creation as a transmigrating jiva. And Ishvara projects another jiva-mind as the devata Varuna.

  

There are two further points here. The jiva while in the role of Varuna abides by His svadharma (as the "governor" and "manifestor" of Water) but also can experience the karmaphala that is due to Him from past human births. Whatever he doesin hisheavenly sojourn (apart from his primary duties) is within the allowance for desire and experience (per his past jiva-karma); his actions as Varuana do not constitute "new karma" that will cause further samsara in a future birth. The future births will be based on unresolved karma of his past humanbirths (and not what He does as Varuna now) and he has to resolve the primal ajnana of Self (and attain 'moksha') ultimately in another human birth. Secondly, this also provides an explanation for why we may find different Varunas of different cycles behaving in different ways (from human-like to full Ishvaratvam). They are different jiva occupants and their minds carry different loads of impressions and ajnana/jnana, and based on this, Ishvara may expand or contract His role through the present Varuna.

  

In the "god is not jiva" view, Varuna's subtle body is a uniqueunitary entity and not a jiva+temporal-Varuna-attributes. This same Varuna is re-projected in the next cycle of creation and He governs accordingto His dharma and participates in the dharmic leela of the Lord. In this dharmic leela, sometimes Varuna appears as the Total Ishvara and sometimes He may appear as if human in behaviour. Thus, instead of a jiva being the base entity that Ishvara may expand into a Varuna or a human, Varuna is the base entity that Ishvara may expand (as all-knowing Godhead) or contract (as if human) as part of His leela. This dharmic leela does not qualify as karma done by Varuna for which he has to experience karmaphala. Rather the human jivas (who alone are capable of generating 'good' or 'bad' karma) learn from His leela among the gods that Ishvara is at the heart of All.

  

We want to understand the world, of ours and the gods, as taught in scripture and by tradition. "What is the knowledge of creation according to Hinduism?" should be an importantquestion for all Hindus. But the real utility of such knowledge is the extent itfacilitates our journey to Self-realization because that is the goal for humanbeings according to ourreligion. From this standpoint, it is OK thatwe have different understandings on creation (even among Hindus) if they can accommodate the curiosity of different human intellects while motivating us in the path of dharma and to the goal of Jnana/Moksha. Whether we are comfortable thinking of gods as jivas or as a separate category, in away we see consistent with shastra and tradition, we want that our bhakti to Ishvara and our commitment to dharma feel enhanced by such knowledge, and that we learn to see Him in All and become attuned to the non-duality of Self.
