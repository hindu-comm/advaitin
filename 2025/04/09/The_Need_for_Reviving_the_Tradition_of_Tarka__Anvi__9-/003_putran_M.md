Namaskaram,

  

This can be seen as a demand vs supply problem.

  

Artha pertains to securing the body; works for creating food, shelter, medicine will have universal demand.

  

Kama though not tied to body survival is a gold-mine for creating demand because of the sense-oriented mind.

  

However, a culture that teaches and organizes society'spursuit of artha-kama around samanya dharma has to be created, developed and nourished by us humans. Then demand for it will be natural, and that can translate into society investing in jobs that are directly for upholding samanya dharma (like charity or humanitarian work). It is not an automatic pull like with artha and kama; it is a choice of instinct that is nourished by knowledge and one learns to align to.

  

What Vishwanath-ji is talking about is how to create a demand for *salaried-jobs* that have to do with upholding Visesha *varnashrama sva-dharma* (applicable here to brahmanas). For this to happen, the larger brahmin community, the larger Hindu community and the kshatriyas (the state and central govt.) will have to know that the brahmana fulfilling his svadharma is necessary and important for the well-being of the whole of society. And when they have such awareness, they will feel the demand for such work to be done and therefore they will ensure that at least the brahmin's basic artha needs are fulfilled.

  

That, we canexpect inan ideal Vedic society - which we don't have now, or within communities that are still *somewhat* aligned to the traditional culture and knowledge. This "somewhat" is the variable. Our vadhyar can survive in his varna-dharma-based life only by doing the home pujas and rituals for people who are themselves ambivalent to their svadharma - having one toe here and the other foot there. These latter are the brahmana-bandhus (of any varna), in the present-day evolution of Hinduism, who follow a vaishya-type dharma to make money in the world (focused on their artha, kama) but also to direct it back to the Vedic traditions, its rigorous institutions and practitioners. They have sort of become the true "vaishyas", "shudras", "kshatriyas" of the day.

  

Is this adaptation within Hinduism sufficient and sustainable? Should we become content with this model or should we still seek something "more original", and how? Chitta-ji seems to think something further needs to happen. That at the deeperlevel of knowledge, there are cracks that these cover-ups and desperate socialadaptations cannot hide for long and we must resolve them through a revival of tarka tradition, to thwart adharmic forces of the present age and reconnect to the knowledge-foundations of oursociety.
