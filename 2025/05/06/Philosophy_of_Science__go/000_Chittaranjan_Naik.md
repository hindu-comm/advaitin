PHILOSOPHY OF SCIENCE



The discipline that represents the state of human knowledge today is science, not philosophy; for it is science that has now come to occupy the dominant position that was once held by philosophy. While science may not be without its critics and detractors, its position as the discipline that spearheads the advancement of human knowledge is so entrenched in modern society that it would be well nigh impossible to dethrone it from the sovereign position it has come to occupy. It is apt therefore that in tracing the trajectory of Western thought, especially of the developments of human knowledge that have led us to the kind of world we live in today, we should give to science the proper place that it deserves in this narrative.

  

We shall here present the development of science not as it is perceived by scientists but as it is considered from a philosophical perspective. Scientists in general are convinced of the methodology adopted by science - for without such a conviction they would not really be scientists – and they have little reason to doubt this methodology given that the theories built by scientists in the last three hundred years have been remarkably successful in explaining the world around us. But philosophers, being philosophers, would be reluctant to accept science in such a blasé manner as scientists do; they would adopt a more critical attitude to science and would seek to know in what way science distinguishes itself from other disciplines of human knowledge and more specifically what it is that makes a scientific explanation more satisfactory when compared to non-scientific explanations so much so that most people today are given to treat only scientific explanations as credible explanations. And they would like to investigate, furthermore, whether the claim of science that it provides an explanation of the world around us really holds true, that is, that these explanations really correspond to the way the world is and the way it operates in reality. A study of science that raises these kinds of questions and undertakes to find answers to them through philosophical investigations belongs to the discipline known as *Philosophy of Science*, a discipline that had its origin in the explorations of the framework of science that philosophers undertook in the early years of the twentieth century. In this section, we shall present an overview of the *philosophy of science* in two parts: the first part being an account of the attempts made by philosophers of science to delineate what it is that defines science and second part being an account of their attempts to investigate the claim that science has been successful in bringing to us knowledge of reality.





PART I : WHAT IS SCIENCE?



The word ‘science’ derives from the Latin word ‘scientia’ which means knowledge. Seen in the light of this etymology, the word ‘science’ should apply to all bodies of human knowledge including philosophy, astronomy, theology, agriculture, sociology and history, as well as the
arts such as poetry, music, painting and the like. But if one were to ask a person today what the sciences are, he or she is likely to mention subjects like physics, chemistry, biology, and metallurgy and exclude disciplines like painting, music and tarot reading. Most people would hesitate to include even sociology and history among the sciences. So, what exactly is science? And what is a scientific theory? Even among the theories that claim to explain the physical world, not all theories would qualify to be called scientific theories or to be included under the umbrella of science. We may still refer to Aristotle’s theories of the universe as science, using the word ‘science’ in the broadest sense of its meaning, but hardly anyone today would classify these theories as scientific theories; whereas they would have no hesitation in classifying the theories of Copernicus, Galileo, Kepler and Newton as scientific theories. So what is it that makes something a science in the sense of the term as it is used today? Evidently, the word ‘science’ is now used in a restrictive sense to refer to only those disciplines of knowledge that conform to a notion of the word as it has evolved from the time of the Scientific Revolution.



The foundation for the modern scientific worldview was laid by the Renaissance polymath, Nicolaus Copernicus (1473-1543). In 1542, Copernicus challenged the geocentric model of the universe and proposed a heliocentric model of the universe. The geocentric model, also known as the Ptolemaic system, was an essential part of the old Aristotelian worldview and it was unchallenged for 1800 years until Copernicus proposed the alternate system. The Copernicus’ theory was strongly opposed by the Christian Church as it went against the orthodox position of the Scriptures, which propagated a fixed-earth view of the universe. In 1616, the Church banned all books that advocated the motion of the earth. But not withstanding these strictures, the Copernican model gained ground and, within 100 years, became the new scientific orthodoxy. Clearly, the Copernican model offered a simpler and more elegant way of explaining the movement of the planets in the sky than had the Ptolemaic system. The Copernican system soon led to the development of modern physics through the works of Johannes Kepler (1571-1630) and Galileo Galilei (1564-1642). Historians regard Galileo as the first modern physicist. He was the first to demonstrate that the language of mathematics could be used to describe the behaviour of material bodies; he was also the first to lay emphasis on testing hypotheses against experimentation. These two methodical elements, which were to become the hallmark of the scientific method, were most eminently employed by Newton whose main work, the Principia published in 1687, was to mark the culmination of the Scientific Revolution. Newton’s work provided the framework for science for the next 200 years. But what exactly was this framework? What were its foundations?



THE PROBLEM OF DEFINING WHAT SCIENCE IS



To gain an appreciation of the philosophical roots of the scientific framework, we would have to turn to the English philosopher, Francis Bacon (1561-1626), who is generally regarded as the pioneer of the scientific method. Bacon was a great proponent of the new empiricism that emerged from the early achievements of modern science and he was inspired to draw up an ambitious plan to produce a comprehensive work under the title *Instauratio Magna*, or *The Great Instauration*, to describe the new scientific enterprise. The work was never completed, but it contained important sections which lay the groundwork for defining the scientific method. The first part of the book contains a division of the sciences; it was a kind of project that had not been undertaken since the time of Aristotle. In the second part appears the *Novum Organum*, or the *New Instrument*, which gives “true directions concerning the interpretation of nature”. It is Bacon’s account of the scientific method, or as he calls it, the correct method of acquiring knowledge, a method with which his name has come to be lastingly associated.                              
                                   
             

  

Bacon vehemently opposed knowledge based on appeal to authority. He argued that the method of Aristotelian Scholasticism, which was prevalent during his day, was inherently barren and fruitless. He attacked Aristotelian science on the ground that its attempts to derive substantial conclusions from premises that it held to be intuitively evident could not be relied upon since the axioms it takes to be evident are neither obvious nor indisputable. His most notable disagreement with Aristotelian Scholasticism, a disagreement which clearly set the direction for the scientific method, was his belief that knowledge is not a process of preservation of received tradition as held by the Scholastics but one of accumulation through the process of discovery. Bacon was living in a time when new worlds were being discovered on earth and the time was ripe for him to propound the idea that all knowledge had not been revealed by the Scriptures or by Aristotle but that man must set himself on the road to discover new knowledge through the scientific method. The new scientific method he proposed sought knowledge through the twin processes of observation and logical induction in contrast to the Aristotelian method which drew conclusion from fixed axioms. Bacon compared Aristotle’s method to a spider’s web which is drawn out of its own entrails to the scientific method (scientia operative) he proposed which, he said, was like a bee that collects natural matter from the flowers and works them up to produce honey which is healthy and nutritious. The observations of nature which Bacon advocated were not simple observation but were interventionist in that he sought to discover correlations in nature by setting up experiments using instruments like telescopes, microscopes, etc., to aid the senses in uncovering properties and their correlations that were not ordinarily discernable.



But Bacon recognized that in our pursuit of knowledge there are many hindrances, born out of our false opinions and prejudices, which obstruct the acquisition of knowledge of the natural laws. These hindrances tend to ‘anticipate’ nature rather than to explain it truly as it operates in reality. Bacon came up with the metaphor of the *‘idol’* to refer to the hindrances that lead to error in the pursuit of knowledge. He identified four idols that make us susceptible to error. The *idols of the tribe* are intellectual tendencies that are universal to mankind, the most common of which is to search for purposes in nature and to read our own desires into them. The *idols of the cave* are predispositions of particular individuals based on their idiosyncrasies born out of their education, social influence and favoured authorities. The *idols of the marketplace*, which Bacon regards as the most dangerous of all predispositions, is the common but misleading use of language which often leads to superficial distinctions as well as carries with it the potential to embroil men in meaningless verbal disputations. The fourth and last of the idols is the *idol of the theatre* which is essentially the dependency on received theories and their influence; Bacon regards the authority of these theories to be based merely on their ingenious verbal constructions.



The acquisition of genuine knowledge depends not on being led by these predispositions and the anticipations they cause in us but on the discovery of the natural laws. To freely undertake an intellectual exploration to discover the natural laws, one must dispossess the mind of the idols and seek knowledge based purely on the scientific method of experimentation and inductive reasoning. Bacon also considered mathematics to be an auxiliary tool of the scientific method but he was not clear on its use. His main contribution – the core of his philosophy of science – is the method of inductive reasoning elaborated in the second book of the *Novum Organum*. The inductive method aims to arrive at generalizations from observed empirical cases. In order to avoid faulty generalizations, Bacon proposed a method of ‘gradual ascent’, through the careful accumulation of well-founded generalizations of gradually increasing degrees of generality. But the induction he proposed was required to work not only by simple enumeration but also by the non-existence of the negative instance; for Bacon realized that while a generalization is only weakly confirmed by observing that *‘all instances of A are B’*, it is conclusively shown to be false by observing that ‘*a single instance of A is not B’*. He devised tables showing ‘presence’, ‘absence’ and ‘degree’ to facilitate legitimate generalizations and to infer proportionate variations. What survives the process of generalization (through presence) and elimination (due to absence) may be taken as true generalization. And with regard to degree, where the object or property is present to some greater or lesser degree, we must be able to establish the cause or reason for the change. For example, quantitative changes in degrees of heat would be correlated to quantitative changes in the motion of the particles that make up the object in which the heat is observed.



Bacon’s empiricist methodology is strikingly modern, yet it has some deficiencies compared to the method that scientists now employ. For one, Bacon was predominantly preoccupied with the static aspect of the world whereas the science that was making rapid progress even during his day was concerned to a large degree with change. It is in explaining change, the dynamic aspect of nature, that mathematics had become an indispensible tool of science. Bacon failed to see this vital aspect of science. Secondly, Bacon assumed that the properties of the objects science investigates are correlated to other properties that are easily discoverable through observation. He failed to see that there may be unobservable ‘hidden forms’ with which they may be correlated rather than with observable forms. Critics of Bacon have pointed out that this failure on his part prevented him from recognizing the form of the modern scientific hypothesis in which the entities that are postulated are often hidden and unobservable (even through experiments); on this account, they hold his view of the scientific methodology to be naive with regard to the postulation of causes as well as for failing to see that the causes could be far removed in space and time from the effects.



If we should make up for the deficiencies in the methodology conceived by Bacon, it would be quite comparable to the methods used by contemporary scientists which is marked by the postulation of an hypothesis, its formulation in the form of a mathematical equation, the verification of the hypothesis through experimentation, and the use of inductive reasoning underpinning both the formulation and verification of the hypothesis. Can we say then that this method defines what science is? While the method does provide a plausible description of the scientific framework, there is a fundamental problem that stands in the way of accepting it as a precise definition of science. The problem has to do with the method of inductive reasoning which scientists employ in order to arrive at the general laws of nature. When scientists observe certain correlations in nature, invariably and without exception on a sufficient number of occasions, they consider these correlations to be exhibiting universality, that is, of them constituting invariable laws of nature. This seems like a sensible way of establishing the laws of nature and of forming beliefs about the world in general. But this way of ‘discovering’ the laws of natural fails to take into account the objection raised by the Scottish philosopher David Hume.



As we’ve seen in Chapter 2, Hume argued that the use of induction cannot be justified at all. Even if a correlation between two things were to be observed on a great number of occasions, there would be no certainty that it would recur in the future. It is mere force of brute habit that makes us assign a causal connection between them, says Hume. Nobody has seen the mysterious power of an object by which it causes an effect to arise in another object. And we believe that a correlation observed in the past will recur in the future because we presuppose that there is uniformity in nature. But by what means do we know that this uniformity assumption is true? There is no way to prove its truth, and we can easily conceive a world where nature is not uniform but changes its course from day to day. Since the conception of such a non-uniform world is possible, a non-uniform universe cannot be held to be logically impossible. Therefore, the a priori assumption of uniformity in nature is not justifiable. Neither can it be derived from empirical observation since it would simply beg the question. For, we cannot appeal to the fact that nature has behaved largely uniformly up to now to argue that it will continue to be behave the same way because this assumes that the pattern observed in the past will recur in the future – which is nothing but the assumption of uniformity in nature. Philosophers are generally agreed that there is no satisfactory answer to Hume’s objection.



***Popper’s Definition***



How then may science be defined if the method of inductive reasoning which underpins the scientific framework should itself be called into question? That science relies heavily upon induction seems obvious to most of us. But the Austrian born British philosopher Karl Popper (1902-1994) denied this. Indeed Popper’s principal contribution to the philosophy of science lay in his rejection of the idea that it is induction which constitutes the primary method of reasoning employed in the empirical sciences. In his book, *The Logic of Scientific Discovery*, he argues that scientific hypotheses are *deductively validated* through what he called the ‘*falsifiability criterion*’. According to Popper, a scientist seeks to discover an observed exception to his postulated rule and that the absence of contradictory evidence becomes the ground for the corroboration of his theory. One may recall Bacon’s argument that while a generalization is only weakly confirmed by observing that *all instances of A are B*, it is conclusively shown to be false by observing that *a single instance of A is not B*. And to conclude from a given statement ‘*this A is not B*’ to the conclusion that ‘*all A are not B’*’ would amount to a deductive inference and not an inductive inference. Thus, argued Popper, scientists would only need to rely upon deductive inference to corroborate their theories through the falsifiability criterion.



Popper argued, furthermore, that it is the presence or absence of the falsifiability criterion that distinguishes science from pseudo-science. He held falsifiability to be a fundamental feature of a scientific theory. A scientific theory must make some definite prediction which is capable of being tested against experience and to be shown to be false, if it should be so. In Popper’s view, those theories that fail to satisfy this condition do not deserve to be called science, and may be labelled as pseudo-science. Popper’s favourite examples of pseudo-science were Freud’s psychoanalytic theory and Marx’s theory of history. Freud’s theory, for example, could be reconciled with any kind of empirical finding whatsoever. It abstains from laying down any criterion by which its validity may be tested and falsified and uses such vague concepts as repression, sublimation, unconscious desires, etc, in the theory, which concepts were malleable enough to be rendered compatible with any kind of clinical data that would turn up. The theory was thus not falsifiable in any manner whatever. The same was the case with Marx’s theory. When the Marxist claim that capitalism in industrialized societies would give way to socialism and ultimately to communism did not happen, the proponents of Marxism resorted to proffering such arguments as that the inevitable progress of society to communism had been temporarily halted or slowed down on account of the rise of the welfare state and the consequent ‘softening’ of the proletariat, etc. These kinds of ad hoc arguments allowed Marx’s theory to be made compatible with any possible course that human history was capable of taking and thus rendered it impossible of being definitely falsified. According to Popper, therefore, neither of these two theories qualified to be called a scientific theory. In contrast, he said, Einstein’s theory of gravitation made a very definite prediction that light from the distant stars would be deflected by the gravitational field of the sun; and this was later confirmed by observations made by the English astrophysicist Arthur Eddington during the solar eclipse of 1919. Had it been shown that the light from the stars were not deflected by the presence of the sun, Einstein’s theory would have been falsified. Einstein’s theory thus satisfied the criterion of falsifiability.



At the surface, Popper’s thesis appears plausible but a closer examination reveals that it has two fundamental weaknesses. The first weakness pertains to the argument he puts forth that science can get by without the use of induction. This would have been true had the goal of science been merely to refute theories, but this is not always the case. Scientists may indeed have the aim of showing that the theories of their rivals are false but it is more often the case that they would like to establish their own theories; and for this they would need to employ inductive reasoning. Take for instance the hypothesis that all metals conduct electricity. Popper’s thesis revolves around the basic idea that even if we show that every piece of metal we examine conducts electricity, it would not prove that the hypothesis is universally true; whereas if a scientist discovers that even one piece of metal fails to conduct electricity, it would conclusively show the hypothesis to be false. However, the hypotheses scientists propose are often not of this kind. They are more often of the following kind: ‘some metals conduct electricity and the electric current generated between two nodes in a piece of conducting metal is proportional to the potential difference between the two nodes’. To establish a theory of this kind, scientists cannot get by without the use of inductive reasoning. While it is possible that the theory may one day be shown to be false, the establishment of the theory does not wait for such an eventuality to happen as it may entail a period of endless waiting. In actual practice, scientific theories are established on the basis of them having been demonstrated to be true in all the cases examined so far notwithstanding the possibility that the theory may be discredited at some future date if it should turn out that some cases are not compatible with the theory. To establish a theory even on such a provisional basis would require the use of inductive reasoning; inductive reasoning thus forms an essential part of the scientific framework.



The second weakness of Popper’s thesis is that the application of the criterion of falsifiability to distinguish science from pseudo-science is, in practice, rather problematic. The history of science is replete with cases where respectable scientists have routinely followed the very methods that Popper accused Freudians and Marxists of adopting in order to hold on to theories that should have been discarded in the first instance if the criterion of falsifiability had been brought to bear on them. For example, Newton’s gravitational theory made certain definite predictions about the paths that the planets would follow as they orbit the sun. This was generally borne out by observation. But there was one planet, Uranus, whose observed behaviour did not conform to the path predicted by Newton’s theory. But instead of discarding the theory on the ground that it had been falsified, two scientists of the nineteenth century, Adams and Leverrier, independently suggested that there was a planet, yet to be discovered, which was exerting additional gravitational force on Uranus. Shortly thereafter, the planet Neptune was discovered in almost exactly the location where Adams and Leverrier had predicted it would be in. Were Adams and Leverrier not adopting the very tactic that Popper had criticized Freudians and Marxists of adopting? They took a theory which should have been discarded if the falsifiability criterion had been applied to it but instead attempted to explain away the conflicting observation by postulating the existence of a new planet. Isn’t this similar to what Marxists did when capitalism continued to thrive instead of giving way to communism? They did not discard Marxism but stuck to the theory and tried to explain away the conflicting observation by postulating other causes for the deviation. In view of the kind of situation that prevails in actual practice, critics of Popper have been quick to point out that his attempt to demarcate science from pseudo-science cannot be right. But the criticism seems rather harsh considering that science is bound by a discipline that requires the scientific hypothesis to be formulated in a manner that clearly specifies the empirical criteria by which it may be experimentally validated or falsified whereas theories of the kind advanced by Freud and Marx are not constrained by such a discipline. A scientific theory always carries with it the criterion by which it can be unequivocally rejected even if its rejection should be provisionally held in abeyance to check out the validity of new explanations that may account for the observed deviations from predicted outcomes whereas the absence of such a criterion in pseudo-scientific theories allow the proponents of the theories to endlessly proffer nebulous explanations, which can neither be verified nor falsified, to account for every instance of conflict of the theory from observed facts.



In contemporary culture, the presence of the falsifiability criterion is held to be the indispensible mark of a scientific theory. It is often cited as the measure by which a theory may be classified as science or non-science. Though it mostly goes unstated, it would be in order to point out here that the falsifiability criterion has to be one that is *empirically* falsifiable. This implies that a scientific explanation has to necessarily be a naturalistic explanation. While the question of the distinctive mark of science by which it may be separated from non scientific disciplines may thus be regarded as settled, the question of the validity of the inductive method of reasoning which scientists use, and which is an indispensible part of science, remains open to question since there appears to be no satisfactory answer to the objection raised by Hume.



***Inference to Best-Explanation (Abductive Reasoning)***



Deductive reasoning is a safe way of reasoning because it allows us to reason from premises which are true to conclusions which would, with certainty, be true because the process of deductive reasoning preserves the truth, without any loss of truth-value, from the premises to the conclusions. But, if Hume should be right, inductive reasoning, the foundation on which science is built, would never provide us with this kind of indubitable certainty that its conclusions are true. The foundations of science would thus appear to be not as unshakable as we might have hoped it to be. But scientists would hardly consider such lack of absolute certainty with regard to the conclusions of science to be a deficiency inasmuch as science, they would say, is not a discipline of knowledge that aims to arrive at a final truth that would not remain open to further questioning. We may note that Bacon had described the scientific method as one of accumulation rather than preservation and that the discovery of even one observation that ran counter to a theory would falsify it. Thus, the scientific framework is one which allows for its theories to always remain open to the possibility of them being falsified by new discoveries and thereby for them to be replaced by new theories which would better explain the observations of nature. This approach of science is what is termed as the positivist approach; it is not an approach that seeks the final truth. To repeat Comte’s words, it is the “*connaissance approchee*”; it comes closer and closer to the truth without ever reaching it with finality. The idea of absolute truth does not fit into the framework of science. Therefore, the inability of inductive reasoning to provide us with a truth that would hold true for all time does not pose a major problem to science. It is sufficient that scientific theories be built on observed correlations which have until now held true to provide scientists with the reason to treat them as valid scientific theories.



Rather than refer to the kind of inference used in science as inductive reasoning, it would be more appropriate to call it *inference to best explanation*. This is because the type of inference used in science often does not fit into the pattern of inductive reasoning. In inductive reasoning, the premise has the form ‘*all observed A have been B*’’ and the conclusion the form ‘*therefore other As are also B’*. Inductive reasoning leads us to infer, for a given kind, unobserved instances from observed instances. But inference-to-best-explanation need not always follow this form of reasoning. It can be any hypothesis which, if true, provides the best explanation for the observed evidence. This manner of reasoning is also called *abductive reasoning* and it subsumes such forms of reasoning as the following one:



The rear window of my neighbour’s car is broken, and

the purse in the back seat is found to be missing.

Therefore, the purse has been stolen by a thief.



This type of reasoning does not fit into the classical pattern of inductive reasoning. Neither does it conform to the schema of deductive reasoning in which the premises entail the conclusion; for it is possible that a stone somebody threw into the air accidentally broke the window and then a bird entered the car and flew away with the purse. But in the circumstances, the combination of the stone-hypothesis and bird-hypothesis turning out to be true seem very remote. The hypothesis that a thief broke into the car and stole the purse would certainly appear to be the more reasonable supposition. While we cannot be certain that this hypothesis is true, it is, on a balanced consideration, the most plausible of the possibilities and is therefore a good example of inference-to-best-explanation.



Scientists frequently employ inference-to-best-explanation as a useful device in building their theories. A good illustration of inference-to-best-explanation is provided by the arguments advanced by Darwin in proposing the theory of evolution. He argued that the observed facts of the living world would be hard to explain if we assume that different species have been created separately and simultaneously whereas it would make good sense to regard the various species that currently inhabit the earth as having descended from common ancestors through gradual evolution, as propounded by his theory. Another example of inference-to-best-explanation is the argument provided by Einstein’s hypothesis for explaining Brownian motion, the random movement of particles suspended in a fluid. While there were a number of explanations advanced in the nineteenth century to account for the phenomenon, Einstein’s hypothesis based on the kinetic behaviour of atoms was accepted as the explanation that best explained it. Having said all this, a question pops up: When there are multiple explanations that explain a phenomenon, what is the criterion by which one may decide which of them provides the best explanation? It seems appealing to consider simplicity and parsimoniousness as indispensible marks of the explanation that we consider to be the best one but this brings up another question. Is there any compelling reason to believe that nature would behave in such a way as to make it appear the simplest in the eyes of human beings? There is no definite answer to this question.



One may see that inductive reasoning is subsumed under the method of inference-to-best-explanation that is used by scientists. While inductive reasoning may not provide us with conclusions whose truths we can hold to be true with apodictic certainty, it can still provide us with not only the best explanation under the given circumstances but also one which we can say with a good measure of confidence as being highly probable. The notion of probability is useful in giving us a picture of how scientific inference works for we may consider the rational degree of confidence that we confer upon a scientific hypothesis, given the evidence in its favour, to be a kind of probability. This would be a number between 0 and 1. In the field of science, we may call the degree of confidence that a scientist has in a hypothesis, in the light of the evidence available to date, as the scientist’s ‘*credence*’ in the hypothesis. Now, if some new evidence should come to light, the probability number can be computed to provide us with the new degree of confidence - the new credence - that the scientist would have in the theory. If the new evidence supports the theory, the new credence would be a higher number - a number closer to 1 - than the previous probability figure had been. The general rule by which the confidence in a theory may increase or decrease incrementally can be expressed by what is called, in probability theory, the *rule of conditionalization*. The rule of conditionalization may be illustrated by a simple example. Suppose we have a pack of cards and we have the hypothesis that the card drawn from it is a queen of hearts. What is the initial credence in the hypothesis? It is 1/52 for there are 52 cards in the pack and they are all equally likely to be drawn. But suppose we had some information which told us that the chosen card was definitely a heart. How would the probability figure be updated in the light of this information? It would be recomputed to the figure 4/52 or 1/13 because we know that there are 4 hearts in the pack of 52 cards. This is how the rule of conditionalization works. It is the derivation of the probability which obtains from a certain conclusion being a consequent on the condition of a certain premise being the antecedent. The rules for updating by conditionalization are dealt with in a branch of statistics known as Bayesian statistics.



The Bayesian rule of conditionalization sheds light on some important aspects of the positivist method of science. While the Bayesian perspective seems to offer a neat explanation of how scientific theories make progress, it has some limitations. The first limitation is that many new scientific discoveries are made on the basis of insights that fit neither into the classical forms of reasoning nor into the Bayesian model of scientific advancement. Bayesian conditionalization, which describes how a scientist’s credence in a theory changes when new evidences come to light, implies that the theory has already been in existence. It fails to account for the kind of reasoning that goes from given data to the postulation of entirely new theories. Another limitation of the Bayesian view is that it is limited to the procedure by which we may derive the new credence in a scientific theory when fresh evidences come to light but it does not set a rule by which we may set the initial credence for a hypothesis. In the case of the card example, this was simple enough because we knew that a pack of cards has 52 cards but in real life situations, there are, more often than not, no objective bases to arrive at the credence value. It is therefore difficult to prevent the intrusion of a subjective element into the setting of the initial credence value and this in turn would render the entire successive credence computations dependent on the initial subjective assessment of the credence value. It is difficult to see how this undesirable situation can be avoided.



***Scientific Explanation – Carl Hempel’s Covering Law Model***



It was mentioned earlier in this section that we live in a world today where most people are inclined to treat only scientific explanations as credible explanations. But what exactly is a scientific explanation? And why should people regard it as a more satisfactory way of explaining phenomena? These are question that have engaged the attention of philosophers ever since science emerged as the dominant discipline of human knowledge. One of the most famous accounts of what constitutes a scientific explanation was provided by the German philosopher, Carl Gustav Hempel (1905-1997). His account is known as the *covering law model* of scientific explanation.



Obviously science seeks to provide explanations for the questions we ask about the world. In general, they are responses to the questions which seek to know *why* some phenomenon or aspect of the world is the way it is. For these responses to qualify as scientific explanations, they would need to explain the *why-questions* that spring in our minds about the world in a satisfactory manner. Hempel suggested that what makes a scientific explanation satisfactory is that it fits into the logical structure of a deductive argument in which the conclusion follows from a set of premises that are empirically known to be true. The conclusion of a scientific explanation may thus, quite logically, be regarded as true. In other words, the conclusion being the phenomenon to be explained, the scientific explanation provides a logical schema by which it follows from the premises, since the premises entail the conclusion, and the set of premises it follows from, being based on empirical facts, tells us why the conclusion is true. For example, in order to answer the question *why grass is green,* we would need to construct an argument whose conclusion would be ‘*grass is green’* and whose premises would tell us why this conclusion is true. The phenomenon to be explained is called the *explanandum* and the premises that do the explaining are called the *explanans*.



But that is not all there is to Hempel’s account of scientific explanation. Hempel held that in addition to the condition that the premises should be true ones, at least one of the premises, the explanans, should be a general law of nature. Thus a scientific explanation appeals not just to premises that are true but also to a general law of nature from which the conclusion follows logically. In other words, the conclusion needs to be covered under a general law of nature. And this is what lends to Hempel’s account of a scientific explanation the name ‘*covering law’*. It is also called the *deductive-nomological* explanation (nomology being the science of laws and law-making). Thus, a scientific explanation may appeal to both general laws as well as to particular facts but it needs to include at least one general law in the explanans or the set of premises. In other words, the essence of a scientific explanation is to show that the phenomenon to be explained is covered under a general law of nature. And this is what makes the explanation satisfactory according to the proponents of science – it takes the mystery away from the phenomenon by showing that it is a natural consequence of a general law of nature. For example, after Newton explained why the planets move around the sun in elliptical orbits by showing that it can be deduced from the general law of gravitation along with some supplementary facts, there was no more a mystery about why the planets move in elliptic orbits around the sun.



Most scientific explanations fit well into Hempel’s covering law model of explanation. However, the model has some problems. The problems relate to cases that fit into Hempel’s model but which would fail to count as genuine scientific explanations. These problems have been classified into two kinds known as the *problem of symmetry* and the *problem of irrelevance*.



To illustrate the *problem of symmetry*, let us take the example of a man’s shadow cast on the ground by the slanting rays of the sun. If we would like to know why the shadow is 8 feet long, the explanation from the covering law model would take some form such as the following: (i) the man is 6 feet tall, (ii) light rays travel in straight lines, (ii) the length of the shadow cast by the sun follows trigonometric laws, and (iv) the sun is at an elevation of 37 degrees; hence it follows that the length of the shadow is 8 feet. But let us invert the question and ask why the man is 6 feet tall. Now, one could use this very same covering law model to ‘explain’ why he is 6 feet tall by laying down the premises (i) the length of the shadow is 8 feet, (ii) light rays travel in straight lines, (ii) the length of the shadow cast by the sun follows trigonometric laws, and (iv) the sun is at an elevation of 37 degrees; hence it follows that the man is 6 feet tall. But is this explanation a valid response to the why-question which seeks to know why the man is 6 feet tall? Surely the man is 6 feet tall due to a variety of reasons ranging from his genetic make-up, the kind of environment in which he grew up, etc, and not because of the four premises listed here above.



The *problem of irrelevance* occurs due to explanations which fit into the covering law model but which are irrelevant to the explanandum or thing to be explained. For example, if we should want to know why John is not pregnant, one could provide an explanation, perfectly compliant with the covering law model, laying down the premises (i) that those who take birth control pills do not become pregnant, and (ii) John has been taking birth control pills believing they are multivitamin pills, and claim that the conclusion ‘John is not pregnant’ follows logically from the truth of these two premises. But such an explanation misses the main point due to which John is not pregnant, namely that he is a man and men don’t become pregnant.



Some philosophers have rejected the covering law model of explanation on the ground that it fails to take causality into account. For example, the covering law model runs into a problem in each of the two cases cited above because it attempts to provide an explanation without reference to causality. These philosophers therefore prefer a causality-based account of explanation in favour of a covering-law account of explanation. The two are in many cases similar but causality based accounts avoid those kinds of problems that surfaced in the examples cited above. But explanations based on causality based accounts have a different problem to contend with: it has to overcome the objections raised by Hume. For according to Hume, causality is something that is never experienced – certainly no one has seen the hidden power by which one thing may cause another thing to occur – and the very notion of causality, he said, is something we project on to the events of the world out of sheer habit. We can appreciate why Hempel had steered clear of the notion of causality in providing his account of scientific explanation; he was an empiricist to the core, being one of the last eminent figures of the logical positivism movement, and he would have gone against the grain of empiricism to include a metaphysical entity like causal power in his account. Many logical empiricists even denied that there are things like atoms, gravitational fields and magnetic fields, referring to them as fictional metaphysical entities and not entities that actually existed in nature. So, those who seek to provide a causal-based account of scientific explanation would need to provide an effective counter to Hume’s objection for their thesis to stand on a firm footing. So far, this has not been forthcoming.



A second problem with causal-based accounts is that some of the things called causes in science are not real causes. Take for example the scientific answer to the question seeking to know what water is or what temperature is. When chemists discovered water to be H2O, it is believed they gave us an explanation of what water is; or when physicists discovered temperature to be the aggregate kinetic energy of the particles, it is believed they gave us an explanation of what temperature is. But in both these cases, the explanation is not really causal. After all, H2O is not the cause of water; it is what *being water* is. Likewise, the aggregate kinetic energy of the particles in a fluid is not the cause of the fluid having the particular temperature it has; it is what the *being of that temperature* is. In both these cases, the statements of causes do not point to real causes; they are identity statements disguised as portraying causes. These may be regarded as cases of *scientific reduction*. It is obvious that the causal account of scientific explanation has its own set of problems to deal with.



***Explanation, Scientific Reduction and Multiple Realizability***



Scientists often resort to *reduction* in the course of providing explanations*.* But what exactly is reduction? An entity is said to reduce to another entity when it is considered to have no ontological existence over and above the reduced entity. Thus when water reduces to H2O or temperature reduces to aggregate kinetic energy of the constituent particles, water and temperature would be considered to have no ontological existence over and above H2O or aggregate kinetic energy of the particles respectively. Reduction may also be looked upon as a specific kind of assimilation in which the object under study is assimilated into a set of dissimilar traits. Reduction is held to be eliminative inasmuch as it eliminates the ontological existence of the things that are reduced. In the field of science, reductionists are generally realists who hold physical matter to be the only kind of thing that exists. But reductionists need not necessarily be physicalists; they can be committed to the reality of any kind of reducing base. Idealists reduce all things to ideas or mind whereas physicalists reduce all mental phenomena to matter. Both idealism and physicalism disagree with Cartesian dualism which is committed to the existence of both mind and matter.



Most contemporary reductionists are committed to materialism or physicalism. It is common among scientists committed to physicalism to reduce all mental and cognitive activities to physiological activities in the brain. Certainly much of the research conducted in fields like neuroscience proceeds on this basis. This kind of reduction where pain, for example, is held to have a being that is of the same type as the being of a certain type of neural firing in the brain is called type-identity reduction. But the position of reductionists has not remained unchallenged. Anti-reductionists argue that this kind of reduction leaves behind an explanatory gap inasmuch as there is always some aspect of the thing reduced that fails to be explained by the reducing physical base. The hard problem of consciousness is a classic case in point; for the reduction of cognitive phenomena to brain activities leaves behind the subjective experience of ‘what it is to be like’, which is part and parcel of conscious experience, to be still explained. Reductionists argue that when some mental state *just is* some physical state then there is nothing left to be explained about how these states connect and that it is sheer misunderstanding on the part of the anti-reductionists to ask for something more than an identity-statement. Anti-reductionists point to the asymmetry of the relation to argue that it cannot be a case of simple identity; for reduction has a directionality (eg, mental states to physical states and not vice versa) which contradicts the notion of simple identity. Moreover, for the concept ‘a’ to be reduced to the concept ‘b’, ‘a’ would need to express a conceptual content that differs from the conceptual content expressed by ‘b’ for the reduction to be operative; and this contradicts the identity thesis. Also notions such as supervenience, which reductionists sometimes use to express the idea of reduction of one thing to another, fail to solve this problem. For if mental states supervene on physiological states, supervenience as an operation would require the mental state to be something over and above the physiological states. Supervenience can express dependency but dependency is not a sufficient condition to effectively claim that there is identity between the two.



One of the problems scientists face is the task of unifying the various disciplines and branches of science. The different branches of science deal with different subjects, having to explain different kinds of phenomena. To explain why metals conduct electricity is a task for physics. To explain why elephants have tusks is a task for biology. To explain why higher interest rates reduce inflation is a task for economics. Each discipline or branch of science specializes in explaining phenomena in its own domain. Now, if we should, like most scientists, hold physicalism to be true, then all branches of science should in some manner reduce to physics since all things in the world are ultimately composed of physical particles. This appears to be a reasonable expectation. Living organisms are made up of cells, which are made up of long chains of molecules which in turn are made up of atoms and sub-atomic particles. Therefore the objects that biologists study are nothing but complex combinations of physical particles. Likewise, economics which studies the behaviour of business organizations and consumers in the marketplace, consist of the organizations of physical things and human consumers, and human consumers are living beings that are ultimately made up of physical particles. So, in principle, if we begin with the premise that physicalism is true, it would entail that all branches of science would ultimately be subsumed under physics.



It shouldn’t come as a major surprise to us that physicists do not actually subscribe to this line of thought. It certainly seems far-fetched to suggest that physics would one day be able to derive the laws of biology or the laws of economics directly from the laws of physics. The actual history of the evolution of these branches of science shows that they are to a large degree autonomous of physics. One way of explaining how these branches can be autonomous of physics even when they remain grounded in physicalism is by making use of the concept of *multiple-realizability*. The *multiple-realizability* thesis was introduced by Hilary Putnam to challenge the brain-state-theorists who claimed that each mental type is identical to some (yet-to-be-discovered) neural type in the brain. For the brain-state-theory to be true, pain, for instance, would have to be some physical-chemical type of thing common to all terrestrial creatures having pain-bearing capacity and which can be correlated exactly with each occurrence of the pain. But neural anatomy differs across different kinds of pain-bearing creatures, especially at the level of circuitry in the brain. Organisms which are not closely related but which have had to adapt to similar environments in their evolutionary history are likely to have different physical-chemical types correlating to pain. Thus, the likelihood of the same physical-chemical type being correlated to pain in all pain-bearing creatures is quite remote. Putnam’s multiple-realizability thesis was originally put forward as an argument against reductionism but it also has its use in explaining how different branches of science can spring from a common physicalist base.



The principle of multiple-realizability may be illustrated by a simple example. Consider the various kinds of chairs that one is likely to find in a furniture shop. Each individual chair in the shop is a physical thing, like everything else in the world. But the physical composition of the chairs and their individual characteristics would be different. Some chairs would be made of plastic, some of wood, some of aluminium, some entirely of cushion, and so on. And they will certainly differ in their shapes, sizes, colours, weights, and other characteristics. It is impossible to define this thing called chair in purely physical terms by stating that it would be a chair if and only if it possesses a certain set of physical characteristics. What this illustrates is that chairs are multiply realized at the physical level. The concept of multiple-realization has been used by philosophers to explain why psychology or biology or economics cannot be reduced to the fundamental physics of particles despite physics being a study of the only kind of thing – matter – that exists. But not all philosophers are happy with the notion of multiple-realizability. In response to the multiple-realizability thesis, some reductionists have claimed that reductions are meant to be domain-specific, and that when we look at type-identity reductions within the specific domains they are meant to operate in, the inconsistency between the thesis of identity-theorists and multiple-realizability evaporates. They also point to the success that neuroscience has achieved by assuming a continuity of underlying neural mechanisms across the nervous system of different species. This wouldn’t have been possible if radical multiple-realizability had been the case as championed by anti-reduction philosophers. And so, the debate continues.
