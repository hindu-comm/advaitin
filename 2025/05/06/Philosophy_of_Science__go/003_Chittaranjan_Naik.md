PHILOSOPHY OF SCIENCE – PART II





DO SCIENTIFIC EXPLANATIONS DESCRIBE REALITY AS IT IS?



Most people would have little reason to doubt that science explains the world to us by informing us about how nature actually operates in reality. For, after all, a scientific explanation wouldn’t qualify to be called a explanation if it were merely spinning a convenient yarn to tell us how a particular phenomenon manifests in nature. It may therefore come as a major surprise to many people that philosophers are actually divided over the question of whether a scientific explanation really corresponds to the way nature operates in reality or whether the explanation is merely a convenient fiction that serves the purpose of helping us predict things in nature. In the field of philosophy of science, the debate over this question is known as the *realism verses anti-realism* debate.



To appreciate the genesis and scope of this debate, one would have to see it against the backdrop of the great philosophical movements, primarily Empiricism, Idealism and Neo-Kantianism, that had swept through Europe in the previous three centuries. Empiricism with its antipathy to treat unobservable entities as existential things, or Idealism with its notion *to be is to be perceived,* strongly dissuaded its proponents from considering the unobservable realm posited by science as a genuine existential world. On the other hand, there were staunch realists who advocated, as Russell did, that we abandon the path of the philosophers - the ‘system-builders’ as they called them - and pursue the more robust path of science, which in 400 years had borne results far more fruitful than 2000 years of philosophy had done. Let us now look at the contours of this debate.



THE SCIENTIFIC REALISM VERSES ANTI-REALISM DEBATE



***The Instrumentalism Thesis***



The *realism verses anti-realism* debate in science is not the same as the *realism verses idealism* debate in philosophy but it has some affinities to it. Within the domain of philosophy of science, realists are those who hold the position that the aim of science is to provide a true description of the world. According to them, a good scientific theory is one which, while explaining the phenomenon to be explained, faithfully describes the way the world operates in manifesting that phenomenon. Anti-realists, on the other hand, hold that the aim of science is to find theories that predict the results of experiments and observations. Scientific theories are theories that serve as instruments for predicting results; anti-realism is, on this account, also called *instrumentalism*. Anti-realists or instrumentalists believe that a scientific theory needs to be *empirically adequate* rather than be a true description of the world.



The conflict between scientific realism and anti-realism comes to the fore most emphatically when scientists postulate theories that make claims about unobservable regions of reality. Realists treat scientific theories which speak of the existence of atoms, electrons, neutrons, quarks, photons, leptons, and other such particles, which are unobservable in the ordinary sense of the word and are quite often simply mathematical derivations, as describing the true features of the world. They argue that the success of the theories offers strong reason to show that the world is as described in them. By virtue of the great success that science has enjoyed in the last four centuries, the realist position has come to be the commonsense position in the world. But nevertheless, anti-realists do not subscribe to this view. They hold that the success of a theory is no measure of the theory being a true description of the world. When scientists built the kinetic theory, its aim was to deduce consequences such as the variations in expansion of gases when variable amounts of heat were applied to them under constant pressure, etc. Whether the gases really contain atoms and molecules in kinetic motion is of no consequence as long as the theory serves the purpose of predicting observations. In other words, the purpose of the theory is exhausted in it being an instrument of predicting observable phenomena; hence *instrumentalism* rather than *realism* is the true nature of science. The anti-realist position in science stems from the philosophical position of empiricism which holds that human knowledge is limited to what can, in principle, be experienced. To step beyond the range of observables is to foray into the land of fiction. Empiricists are not necessarily idealists; they may be realists who are agnostic about the unobservable regions of reality. Many leading scientists of the late nineteenth century and early twentieth century doubted whether atoms and subatomic particles are entities that really exist.



***The No-Miracles Argument and the Anti-Realist Response***



One of the most compelling arguments marshalled by the realists is called the ‘*no miracles argument’*. Realists point out that scientific theories not only make accurate predictions about the world but also facilitate technological innovations which are put to fruitful use. For example, the scientific theories about atoms and electrons and the changes that take place in energy states when electrons jump from one orbit to another have helped develop laser technologies that are useful in making guided missiles, correcting myopias, printing high quality texts and graphics, etc. It would be an astonishing coincidence, stretching the limits of our credibility, if the electron theory should be able to make not only accurate predictions about the world but also enable us to achieve such marvellous technological advances on the basis of mere fictions. If atoms and electrons are just convenient fictions, as the anti-realists proclaim them to be, then what is it that makes lasers work? What is it that makes guided missiles hone on to their targets? The anti-realists who maintain that atoms and electrons are mere fictions are left with no credible explanation to account for how these technologies work or what makes these theories predict phenomena accurately. Being anti-realist would thus amount to being a believer in miracles. But there are no miracles in nature. It is not befitting on our part to believe in miracles when there are non-miraculous explanations available to account for the phenomena.



The purpose of the no-miracles argument is not to make the claim that scientific explanations are absolutely true or that they constitute the final descriptions of reality. The purpose is far more modest: it is to make an appeal to the *principle of inference to best explanation.* The best explanation of the fact that scientific theories are empirically successful and that they enable us to make technological advances of the kind never heard of before, is that these theories are true and that the entities and behaviours of the entities they speak about are just as the theories say they are. Unless this explanation is accepted, the phenomena to be explained, and the fact that technological applications of scientific theories work, would amount to nothing short of miracles. But there are no miracles in reality; hence the only rational choice is to accept that these theories actually describe reality. The no-miracles argument is attributed to the American philosopher, Hilary Putnam.



The response of the anti-realists to the no-miracles argument was to point to the historical fact that many theories which were considered empirically successful during their time, such as the *electric fluid theory* on the basis of which useful inventions like the electric-arc bulb were made, had to be subsequently discarded as false. Another example is the *phlogiston theory* which was used to explain combustion in the seventeenth century. It was believed that when an object burns, it releases a substance called phlogiston into the atmosphere. This theory was quite successful in explaining the empirical data available at that time. But the phlogiston theory had to be eventually discarded in favour of a new theory which explained burning through reaction of things with oxygen. The anti-realists say that the no-miracle argument for scientific realism is too naive and short-sighted as it offers no reason by which we may be assured that the same fate which befell the electric fluid theory and the phlogiston theory would not befall the scientific theories of today. Therefore, the no-miracles argument does not hold.



The realists’ counter to the anti-realists’ argument-from-history was to make some refinements to the no-miracles argument. In the new refined argument, they make the weaker claim that empirically successful theories need only be approximately true and not conformant with reality to every last detail. Some realists also proposed that the empirical success of a theory is not to be measured merely by its fit with known data but also by its ability to make predictions about phenomena that are still unknown at the time. The aim of these refinements was to bring all cases of successful empirical theories within the ambit of the no-miracles argument. The refined weaker claim of the realists certainly made it more difficult to find cases of empirically successful theories which were later discovered to be false. But they do not entirely eliminate cases that fail to fit the no-miracles argument. Take for example Christian Huygens *light-wave theory* proposed in the seventeenth century according to which light was emitted in all directions as a series of vibrating waves through the medium of ‘luminiferous aether’ (or ‘ether’ as it is more commonly known). This theory, in its mathematical form formulated by Auguste Fresnel in 1816, was successful in predicting new optical phenomena related to diffraction and interference that were entirely unexpected at that time. But modern physics rejects the notion of ether, so the hypothesis that light consists of wave-like vibrations in ether is now held to be false. The anti-realists argue that even according to the modified criterion of empirical success put forth by the realists, we have here an example of a theory that was empirically successful in its time but which turned out to be false subsequently. The positing of *luminiferous aether*, say the anti-realists, was not even roughly on the right lines; it was just false.



***Where lies the Dividing-Line between the Observable and the Unobservable?***



The distinction between the observable and unobservable regions of reality is central to the realism verses anti-realism debate. The anti-realists, with their strong empiricist stance, do not accept that entities which lie on the unobservable side of the observable-unobservable divide have any reality. These entities are, in the eyes of the anti-realists, imaginary entities. But realists contend that the dividing line between the observable-unobservable realms is philosophically problematical. If the anti-realists should depend on there being a principled way of demarcating the realm of the observable from the unobservable, then they are in trouble because there is no clear and unambiguous demarcation possible between the two in the domain of science. In ordinary parlance, we may speak of tables and chairs as being observable things and atoms and molecules as unobservable things but in the field of scientific exploration, scientists are given to speak, quite justifiably, of observing things such as atoms and sub-atomic particles with the help of sophisticated instruments. These claims of scientific observation cannot be set aside so easily. Consider for example, the scientific observation of a particle in a cloud chamber. When charged particles such as electrons move through the cloud chamber with high velocity, they collide with the atoms in the air and convert them into charged ions around which there occurs condensation of water-vapour causing them to become visible to the naked eye. The trace of the electrons in the cloud chamber can thus be seen or observed. Granted that these observations are not direct observations of electrons but they do constitute visible detection of the electrons. The case is similar to the observation of a jet-airplane through the observation of the visible vapour trail it leaves in the sky. One may argue that the observation of the trail is not the same as the actual observation of the airplane itself. But is it possible to clearly set apart a detection from an observation?



The realists say that the two dimensions of reality, the observable and the unobservable, lie on a smooth continuum and that it is impossible for us to take a principled stand on where the transition from the one to the other lies. An influential argument against anti-realism was advanced by the American philosopher Grover Maxwell (1918-1981) who pointed out that the concept of observable varies with the range of devices available; for many people are unable to observe much without the aid of spectacles. In one of the famous challenges posed to the anti-realists, Maxwell asks us to consider the following events: looking at something with the naked eye, looking at it through spectacles, looking at it through binoculars, looking at it through a low-magnification microscope, looking at it through a high-magnification microscope, and so on. All these lie on a continuum. What is the criterion by which we may decide which of them counts as an observation and which does not? When a biologist looks at a micro-organism through a sophisticated microscope, does he observe the micro-organism or does he detect it like we detect the presence of electrons in a cloud chamber? How sophisticated should an instrument be before it transitions from a case of observing to a case of detection? Maxwell argued that there is no clear and unambiguous criterion by which we may clearly segregate the two. The anti-realist attempt to set apart the observable realm from the unobservable is therefore untenable.



The Dutch American philosopher, Bas van Fraassen, arguing for the anti-realists, says that Maxwell’s arguments do not really affect the position of the anti-realists. The term ‘observable’ may have borderline cases but it does not show that the distinction is itself false. For example, hair loss occurs in gradual degrees and it may be difficult to say with regard to men who have lost their hair to a good degree whether they are truly bald or not, but one would not argue on that account that there is no distinction between bald and hirsute as long as there are clear-cut cases of men who have thick growths of hair on their heads and cases of men who have hardly any strand of hair left on their heads. Bald and hirsute are distinctions that may certainly be made notwithstanding the fact that the dividing line between the two may be sometimes vague or unclear. The unobservable-unobservable distinction cannot therefore be set aside as false. There are clear cut cases of entities that can be observed, such as tables and chairs, and entities that cannot, such as electrons and quarks. Perhaps one may draw a parallel here with the famous philosophical problem of defining precisely when grains of sand turn into a heap, a problem that is known in the field of philosophy as the Sorites problem. While it may certainly pose a problem to say precisely when the accumulation of grains of sand would turn them into a heap, no one would, on that account, say that the distinction between grains of sand and heaps of sand is false. Does Fraasen’s argument show that the distinction between the observable and unobservable merits the significance that the anti-realists accord to it? Does it show that knowledge of unobservable is impossible? Philosophers are not agreed on this point.



***The Underdetermination Argument***



Anti-realists use the principle of *underdetermination* to garner support for their claim that the purpose of a theory is to provide empirical adequacy rather than to provide a true description of the world. The notion of underdetermination revolves around the idea that the evidence available to us is often insufficient to determine which theory may be unequivocally accepted as true. A theory is said to be underdetermined, therefore, when there are multiple mutually incompatible theories that can explain the data. Anti-realists hold that available empirical data can often be explained by multiple theories that differ in their descriptions of the world. If the realist claim that scientific theories provide us with true descriptions of the world should be true, then underdetermination would entail that multiple descriptions of the world are true, which is absurd. Underdetermination therefore goes against the realism hypothesis.



Underdetermination occurs not only in the field of science; it is a more general epistemic problem that occurs in diverse fields. When Descartes wondered whether the world we see is really existent externally or is a phenomenon like the one we see in a dream, the handiwork of an all-powerful evil demon out to deceive us, he was appealing to a form of underdetermination. Or consider Newton’s cosmological model with its theory of gravitation and its laws of motion. Fraassen argued that the same predictions made by this theory would also be made by an alternate theory which assumes that the entire universe, instead of being at rest, is moving at some constant velocity. Being situated within the universe, we have no way of knowing the absolute motion of the universe. We are thus faced with the dilemma of having to choose between Newton’s theory which says the universe is at rest or any one of the alternative theories resulting from the assumption that it is moving at some different constant velocity. The initial work on the epistemic problems arising from underdetermination was done by the French physicist and philosopher of science, Pierre Duhem (1861-1916). The investigation undertaken by Duhem was later extended by the American philosopher, W V O Quine, to show that the problem of underdetermination applied not only to scientific theories but to all our knowledge claims in general. Quine incorporated and further developed the problem of underdetermination as part of a more general theory of human knowledge.



Anti-realists are quick to point out that the postulation of unobservable entities in scientific theories makes them particularly prone to be underdetermined. The predictions made by the kinetic theory of gases which postulates the existence of unobserved atoms and molecules in rapid motion, for example, could be achieved, in principle, by other possible theories which conflict with the kinetic theory. The anti-realists argue that there will always be a number of competing theories which can explain the data equally well. In this regard, one may point to the words of the English philosopher, John Stuart Mill (1806-1873), who in his work, *A System of Logic*, had written:



Most thinkers of any sobriety allow, that an hypothesis... is not to be received as probably true because it accounts for all the known phenomena, since this is a condition sometimes fulfilled tolerably well by two conflicting hypotheses..... while there are probably a thousand more which are equally possible, but which, for want of anything analogous in our experience, our minds are unfitted to conceive.



Realists argue that the underdetermination argument is true only in a trivial sense. While there may be more than one theory that explains a given set of data, it is not usually the case that all of them have equal merit. One of them may be simpler than the others or it may have fewer hidden causes or it may offer better coherence with theories in other related areas of science. Another argument the realists put forth is that anti-realists apply the underdetermination argument in a selected manner. For the underdetermination argument would, say the realists, apply to not only unobservable entities of the unobservable world but equally to unobserved entities of the observable world. For example, if a scientist advances the hypothesis that a meteor struck the moon in the year 1950 on the basis of photographs showing a large crater which was not present in photographs taken prior to 1950, the same data can be explained by the hypothesis that a volcano which erupted in the year 1950 caused the crater. The scientist’s hypothesis is here underdetermined by the data even though it is about a perfectly observable entity like the meteor (which was hypothesized to have struck the moon). If we go by the underdetermination argument of the anti-realists, we would have to discard much of the knowledge brought to us by science such as knowledge of the ice age, of dinosaurs that roamed the earth, of the phenomenon of continental drift, etc., as convenient fictions and not as genuine knowledge. Such a conclusion is one that very few philosophers would be willing to accept.



Does this realist argument put an end to the debate? No, it in fact expands the scope of the debate. For when the realists say that not all theories which fit the data have equal merit and things like simplicity, the presence of fewer hidden causes, its coherence with theories in other related areas of science, etc., have to be also considered, it demonstrates the presence of a problem that goes beyond the boundaries of the *scientific realism verses anti-realism debate* to encompasses the overall enterprise of science. For, the cases of underdetermination considered here so far constitute just one type of underdetermination known as *contrastive underdetermination*. Another kind of underdetermination comes into play when factors beyond fit-to-data are called upon to determine the merits of competing theories that equally well explain the data. Underdetermination of this latter kind is called *holist underdetermination*. Holist underdetermination poses a challenge to not just those theories of science that incorporate unobservable entities in them but to all scientific theories in so far as it poses a challenge to the *rationality* of science itself. The preliminary works that introduced holist underdetermination as a major theme in the philosophy of science were due to Duhem and Quine.



Quite simply, what holist underdetermination says is that when scientists test the validity of a theory, the test applies not only to the hypothesis in question but to the entire web of background theories and assumptions that go to support the hypothesis. In other words, the empirical consequence that derives from an hypothesis does not depend on that single hypothesis alone, considered in isolation from others, but from the hypothesis being conjoined with many other beliefs and hypotheses, including background assumptions about the world. When, therefore, the prediction of an hypothesis fails, we cannot know with certainty whether the fault lies with the hypothesis we had set out to test or with one or the other of the many beliefs and hypotheses that were required to hold up and support the hypothesis. In this context, we may revisit the classic example we had cited earlier while discussing Popper’s theory. When Newton’s celestial mechanics failed to accurately predict the orbit of Uranus, scientists did not abandon the theory but challenged the background assumption that the solar system consisted of seven planets. By calculating the location of an hypothetical eighth planet influencing the orbit of Uranus, Adams and Leverrier were eventually led to the discovery of the planet Neptune. But this same strategy failed to work when it was used to explain the deviations in the orbital movement of the planet Mercury. The postulation of an additional planet ‘Vulcan’ hypothesized to be located between Mercury and the sun was not successful in accounting for the variations in Mercury’s movements. A satisfactory explanation of the phenomenon had to await the arrival of Einstein’s general theory of relativity. Duhem had suggested that when the world behaves in a way that does not conform to the expectations set by our scientific theories, we must give up something, but because none of the scientific hypotheses is tested in isolation, no experiment will tell us which hypothesis or belief in the web of our theories and beliefs is the one we must give up. The failed strategy in respect of the attempt to explain Mercury’s movements demonstrates that Duhem was right.



The picture that emerges from all this may be articulated in Quine’s words: “The unit of empirical significance is the whole of science”.
When there is a mismatch between what the web of our theories and beliefs leads us to expect and the actual sensory experience we receive, it becomes an occasion for making a revision in the web of our theories and beliefs but which revision we should make to return the web back to a state of conformity with our experiences is underdetermined by those experiences themselves. Quine argued that it is possible for us to preserve any belief, if we deem it to be sufficiently important to us, by making radical adjustments elsewhere in the web of beliefs. Quine argued that even revisions to basic beliefs about logic and mathematics are, in principle, open to us in the face of recalcitrant experiences. Certain persistent problems posed by quantum mechanics, for example, were solved by rejecting the principle of classical logic, namely the law of excluded middle, and thus making allowance for particles to both *possess* and *not possess* properties such as spatial position and momentum at a given time. The only test of a hypothesis, according to Quine, is whether it fits into our web of connected theories and beliefs as a whole.



Quine held that we are not usually at a loss to decide which of our beliefs we should revise in response to a recalcitrant experience because we are fundamentally disposed to carry out revisions to those beliefs that do the minimum harm to our overall web of beliefs. It is a matter of fundamental psychology, of preserving virtues that are pragmatic in character, than of any criterion that we can unequivocally describe as an objective one for determining what should be discarded and what retained in our web of beliefs when we are confronted with recalcitrant experiences. It is not surprising therefore that underdetermination has been seen by many as posing a threat to the fundamental character of science as a rational enterprise. Philosophers such as Paul Feyerabend have suggested that underdetermination has shown the difference between empirically successful theories and unsuccessful theories to be dependent on factors such as creativity, talent, resolve and resources available at the disposal of those that advocate the theories. In the light of the holist underdetermination thesis, one can see how even theories that bring us knowledge of the ice age, of dinosaurs roaming the earth and of continental drifts are not insulated from being underdetermined by the empirical evidence available at hand.



THOMAS KUHN AND THE STRUCTURE OF SCIENTIFIC REVOLUTIONS



The point of departure for most discussions on underdetermination is Thomas Kuhn’s (1922-1996) book, *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*. Kuhn’s book has had such enormous influence on subsequent developments not only in the discipline of philosophy of science but also in disciplines like sociology and anthropology that it has been rated as one of the hundred most influential books of the twentieth century. Much of Kuhn’s work was a response to the view of science promoted by the logical positivists in the post-war period. For the logical positivists, science was a paradigmatically rational activity, the one assured way of reaching the truth. The logical positivists were *verificationists* and they believed that the rationality of science derived from its theories being subjected to objective empirical tests. It mattered not how a theory was discovered; what mattered was how it was justified. They therefore asserted that philosophers of science should distinguish between the *context of discovery* and the *context of justification* and focus on the latter. The logical positivists believed that disputes between competing theories could be resolved by appeal to neutral observational facts. Their concerted efforts were therefore directed towards finding the objective criteria by which scientific theories could be verified and more specifically towards finding ways to characterize neutral observational facts. But the failure to find a clear separation between observational facts and scientific theories ultimately led to the demise of the logical positivist movement. Kuhn’s work on scientific change and scientific revolutions needs to be seen against the backdrop of these historical developments.



***Paradigms, paradigm shifts and scientific revolutions***



Kuhn disagrees with the logical positivists that the study of philosophy of science would be well-served by discarding the context of history and focusing on the context of justification. He begins the first chapter of his book by making a case for the role of history in determining the actual nature and practice of science. A study of history, he says, can produce a decisive transformation in the image of science that we have been possessed with and which has been formed by reading about ‘finished scientific achievements’ as they have been recorded in the classics and science textbooks. He says that we have been misled by these texts in fundamental ways and that a historical study of the research activity of science can provide us with quite a different concept of science.



Scientific ideas and theories change. The theories that are prevalent today in almost every area of science are not the same as they were 50 years ago and are most likely very different than what they were 100 years ago. Most changes in scientific theories take place within the sphere of the normal activity that scientists engage in from day to day. This sphere of scientific activity is what Kuhn called ‘normal science’. But sometimes the change is of such magnitude that the central ideas which formed the backbone of the scientific culture within which scientists worked had themselves to change and be replaced with radically new ideas. Such periods of great upheaval when the entire worldview of science had to undergo a change are what Kuhn called ‘scientific revolutions’. The Copernican revolution in astronomy, the Darwinian revolution in biology and Einsteinian revolution in physics are examples of scientific revolutions.



Central to the practice of what Kuhn calls ‘*normal science’* is the notion of *paradigm*. A paradigm, according to Kuhn, is a disciplinary matrix which shares two main characteristics. The first consists of the body of accepted theory which includes the theoretical assumptions that are shared by all members of the scientific community along with exemplars which inform us about how specific scientific problems have been solved by means of those theoretical assumptions. These are well laid out in the textbooks of the various disciplines of science. The second characteristic is the open-endedness which it permits for pursuing future research within the broad parameters defined by the shared community of scientists. It is the study of the paradigm which prepares the student for membership within the scientific community with which he will come to practice. Because he will there join other members who learned the fundamental features of their fields from the same bases, he will become part of a community that shares the same rules and standards for scientific practice. This shared commitment and the broad consensus it produces among the members of the community, says Kuhn, are the prerequisites for normal science and for the continuation of the research traditions in their respective fields. It lends a broad directionality to the tradition deriving from the constellation of shared assumptions, beliefs and values which unites the scientific community and drives the practice of normal science.



Kuhn asks us to note that the most striking feature of normal science is that its research programs are not aimed at producing major novelties. They seek, rather, to solve problems within the framework of the paradigm and with as little adjustment to the paradigm as possible. Kuhn characterizes the essential nature of normal science as that of *puzzle-solving*. Just as in puzzle-solving, normal science tests the ingenuity and skill of scientists in problem-solving; but it also, at the same time, as in the case of puzzle-solving, anticipates the broad nature of the solution due to the implicit rules it sets to limit the nature of the solution and the steps by which it is to be obtained. In a jig-saw puzzle, for example, any picture produced by arranging the pieces arbitrarily does not count as a solution even if the arrangement should produce a picture more original or more interesting than the one from which the puzzle had been made. To count as a solution, all the pieces of the puzzle must be turned the right side up and must interlock with one another without leaving any gaps in between to produce an intelligible picture. Similar kinds of restrictions set the boundaries for admissible solutions to theoretical problems within a paradigm. In normal science, scientists accept the paradigm unquestioningly, and if a scientist fails to find a solution, it is attributed to the failure of the scientist or to the instrument and not to the paradigm. Normal science does not test the paradigm itself, that is, it aims not at novelties or fact or theories and when successful, it usually finds none.



A period of normal science may last a long time, for decades or even centuries. The puzzle-solving activity during this time is a cumulative enterprise which slowly but steadily extends the scope and precision of scientific knowledge. But in the course of time, anomalies - observations which do not fit the predictions of accepted theories - are discovered. Initially, these anomalies may be ignored or they may be attributed to scientists’ errors or faulty experimental techniques. But certain anomalies persist until they can no longer be ignored. Such anomalies are the precursors to new scientific discoveries and to the invention of new theories. They usually call for adjustments in the theoretical assumptions of the paradigm resulting in *paradigm shifts*. But sometimes, anomalies arise which cannot be accommodated with minor changes to the theoretical base of the paradigm. When anomalies of this kind accumulate and grow into a burgeoning *crisis* within the scientific community, it announces the beginning of a new scientific revolution. This is a period when the old paradigm breaks down and new fundamental theories, calling for radical changes in the paradigm, are advanced. Many alternatives are put forth and there is passionate debate among the scientists until a new paradigm develops. It usually takes a generation or two before all the members of the community are won over to the new paradigm. The scientific revolution then comes to an end.



A number of instances from the history of science can be cited in support of Kuhn’s thesis. The history of science indeed consists of long periods of normal science punctuated by paradigm changing scientific revolutions of the kind Kuhn describes. The Copernican revolution which brought about a transition from Ptolemaic astronomy to Copernican astronomy or the Einsteinian revolution which brought about a transition from Newtonian physics to Einsteinian physics shared the characteristics described by Kuhn to a good degree of accuracy. However, the value of Kuhn’s thesis lay in not just describing scientific revolutions but in certain propositions he put forth regarding the factors that determine the transition to a new paradigm. For in Kuhn’s view, the determinants are not entirely rational, at least not in the sense of what we ordinarily mean by the term ‘rational’. This created quite a stir at the time his book was published. To understand the controversial claims of Kuhn, we would need to look into some troubling features of a paradigm along with certain distinctions we take for granted like the distinction between fact and theory, between discovery and invention.



***The Priority of the Paradigm***                  
                                   
                                   
                                   
                                   
                                   
   An investigation of a paradigm reveals the locus of its commitment through a set of recurrent and quasi-standard illustrations of the theories in its domain in their conceptual, observational and instrumental applications. By studying them and practicing with them, the members of the scientific community learn their craft. While there may remain some penumbral areas of achievements, the recognition of the paradigm of an established scientific community does not pose a difficulty. But the determination of a shared paradigm is not the same as the determination of the shared rules that operate in the paradigm. To discover those shared rules, it would require that we identify the isolable elements, implicit and explicit, which have been abstracted from the research programs of the tradition and been deployed as rules for research. Anyone who has undertaken such a project will discover that it is far more difficult to achieve success in the search for rules than it is to achieve success in the identification of a paradigm. A search for a set of rules that may be driving a given research tradition, says Kuhn, is a source of continual and deep frustration. But that frustration may not be entirely fruitless for it can lead us to the diagnosis of the problem.



“Recognizing that frustration, however, makes it possible to diagnose its source. Scientists can agree that a Newton, Lavoisier, Maxwell, or Einstein had produced an apparently permanent solution to a group of outstanding problems and still disagree, sometimes without being aware of it, about the particular abstract characteristics that make those solutions permanent. They can, that is, agree in their *identification* of a paradigm without agreeing on, or even attempting to produce, a full *interpretation* or *rationalization* of it. Lack of a standard interpretation or of an agreed reduction to rules will not prevent a paradigm from guiding research. Normal science can be determined in part by the direct inspection of paradigms, a process that is often aided but does not depend upon the formulation of rules and assumptions. Indeed, the existence of a paradigm need not even imply that any full set of rules exist.”

(Thomas Kuhn, *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*)



In the absence of a set of well defined rules, what restricts the scientist during periods of normal science to a particular scientific tradition? And how can the tradition even be identified without the essential attributes of it being laid down? Here, Kuhn draws an analogy with the notion of *language games* developed by Wittgenstein in a slightly different context to answer questions like these. According to Wittgenstein, the meaning of a word is not some object to which it corresponds but the use that is made of it in the ‘stream of life’. To counter the classical position that a word is closely correlated with an object, he developed the idea of *language games* to describe the countless ways in which language is actually used in human social interactions. Take the verbal expression ‘Water!’ for example. It can be an exclamation, an order, a request, or an answer to a question. According to Wittgenstein, the word ‘water’ has no meaning apart from its use within a language game. It can be used as an order to have someone bring you water or it can be used to warn someone that the water has been poisoned. Or it can even be used as a secret code by a member of an espionage unit. Though some attributes shared by the instances when we use the word may help us to learn how to use the word, there is no set of attributes that is simultaneously applicable to all members of the class and them alone. The term ‘game’ is used because what we are seeing bears a close ‘family resemblance’ to a number of activities that we have previously learned to call by that name. Something similar is at work here in the case of a scientific tradition, says Kuhn. What they have in common is not some explicit, or discoverable, set of rules and assumptions that gives the tradition its character but some relation that operates by resemblance and by modelling to some part of the scientific corpus which the community has recognized as its established achievements.



Kuhn argues that paradigms are prior to, more binding, and more complete for determining research than any set of rules that could be abstracted from them; that is, they determine normal science without the intervention of discoverable rules. This is the reason why scientific education almost always never begins with the learning of concepts, laws and theories in the abstract and by themselves. Instead they are encountered, from the start, in pedagogically prior units that introduce them through their applications. We may note that a new theory is always announced together with its application to a range of natural phenomena, and that once the theory is accepted these same illustrations accompany the theory into the textbooks from which future practitioners will learn the craft. The point that Kuhn is making here is that normal science can proceed without rules so long as the scientific community in question accepts without question the particular problem-solutions already achieved. Rules become important only when the anomalies that arise in normal science have the potential to cause insecurity about the paradigm itself.



***Worldview transformations, incommensurability and theory-ladenness of data***



The priority of the paradigm has an important bearing on the nature of scientific truth. For it implies that the truth of a scientific theory is not determined by well-defined rules but depends on the paradigm that operates through normal science. In other words, the truths of established scientific theories are dependent on, and relative to, paradigms. In Kuhn’s thesis therefore, it makes no sense to ask for the objective truth of a theory or whether the predictions of a theory corresponds to facts ‘as they really are’. Kuhn suggested that the facts about the world are paradigm-relative. Kuhn espoused a form of anti-realism.



A scientific revolution is the breakdown of the paradigm that had determined the worldview of the scientist. So, when a scientific revolution occurs and the existing paradigm is replaced by a new paradigm, scientists have to abandon the whole conceptual framework they had used to make sense of the world and replace it with another. Kuhn claimed, somewhat metaphorically, that before and after a paradigm-shift, scientists ‘live in different worlds’. Scientific revolutions may therefore be characterized as *worldview transformations*. The kind of crisis that develops at the onset of a scientific revolution, when the old worldview is beginning to break down, is illustrated by the words of Albert Einstein and Wolfgang Pauli spoken when the prevailing scientific worldview of that time was collapsing:



Einstein: “It was as if the ground had been pulled out from under one, with no firm foundation to be seen anywhere, upon which one could have built.”

Pauli: “At the moment physics is again terribly confused. In any case, it is too difficult for me, and I wish I had been a movie comedian or something of the sort and had never heard of physics.”

(Reproduced from Thomas Kuhn’s ‘*Structure of Scientific Revolutions’*)



All crises begin with the blurring of the paradigm and the consequent loosening of the rules for normal research. It would be obvious by now that the transition from a paradigm in crisis to a new paradigm is not a cumulative process achieved by an articulation of the extension of the old paradigm. Rather, says Kuhn, it is “a reconstruction of the field from new fundamentals, a reconstruction that changes some of the field’s most elementary theoretical generalizations as well as many of its paradigm methods and applications”. Some historians have noted the similarity this momentous scientific change displays to the kind of change that occurs in visual gestalt when some pattern on paper that was first seen as a bird is now seen as an antelope. Kuhn however says that the gestalt example does not illustrate the full nature of a paradigm shift; for in the case of the visual gestalt, the subject has the freedom to switch back and forth between the two ways of seeing whereas in the case of scientists belonging to one or the other paradigm, they just see it the way the paradigm dictates. And this brings us to the notion of *incommensurability*.



Two paradigms become *incommensurable* when the differences in their interpretive frameworks render them impossible of being compared in a meaningful way. Proponents of the two paradigms are prone to see things in different ways – they even use the same words with different semantic connotations - so much so that they are liable to talk past each other and fail to establish a meaningful connection. This is because scientific concepts derive their meanings from the paradigm in which they play a role, and they may, on that account, acquire different meanings in the two paradigms depending on the differing roles their conceptions were meant to play in them. Kuhn subscribed to the principle of ‘holism’ which maintains that a part cannot be understood in isolation from the whole within which it is imbedded. To understand the Newtonian concept of ‘mass’, therefore, says Kuhn, we need to understand the whole of the Newtonian paradigm within which alone it obtains its meaning; and this meaning is different than the meaning the term ‘mass’ has in Einsteinian physics. Likewise the meaning of the term ‘space’ in Newtonian physics is different than the meaning it obtains in Einsteinian relativistic physics.



Kuhn uses these points to argue that paradigm-shifts are not fully objective and that the development of a new paradigm is not a progression that takes us closer to an ‘objective truth’. Traditionally, the choice between two competing theories was seen as a determination that followed from an objective assessment of the two in the light of the evidence available at hand. But Kuhn’s incommensurability thesis tells us that such simplistic accounts of paradigm shifts are naive depictions of what takes place during the transition to a new paradigm. Scientific revolutions, that is, the replacement of one paradigm by another, cannot be regarded as the replacement of *wrong* ideas by *right* ideas. Such a characterization would not be correct because there is no objective basis on which a comparison between the two can be carried out. In other words, there is no common framework for evaluating them, nor even a common language in which the two can be meaningfully and objectively compared. In paradigm changes and scientific revolutions, there is no progression towards an objective truth; one is not an objectively truer description of the world than the other; they are just different. This also implies that the winning over of the members of a scientific community to a new paradigm is not entirely based on explicit rational criteria but involves an element of arbitrariness and of the power of persuasion in getting their acceptance to the new paradigm. Kuhn compared it to a conversion experience involving an element of faith. “The transfer of allegiance from paradigm to paradigm”, he wrote, “is a conversion experience which cannot be forced.” Kuhn held that factors like peer pressure and the presence of forceful advocates would have significant influence on the transfer of allegiance.



When Kuhn’s thesis was published, it created a storm in scientific and philosophical circles. People were aghast at the suggestion that science was not entirely a rational activity but involved things like faith and peer pressure in deciding the theories it adopts. One critic is said to have remarked that Kuhn had made theory-choice in science ‘a matter of mob psychology’. Critics also argued that if two paradigms are incommensurable with each other as held by Kuhn, then there would be no real opposition between theories from the two paradigms. But this, they pointed out, is not true. Take, for instance, the proposition that the mass of an object is dependent on its velocity. Newton’s theory says this is false while Einstein’s theory says it is true. Since everybody agrees that the two theories conflict with each other, the incommensurability thesis gets into trouble. But the critics in this case seem to have misunderstood what Kuhn meant by incommensurability since Kuhn admits that the theories of the new paradigm gain currency on the basis of them having better explanatory power in respect of the anomalies that had led to a crisis in the old paradigm. In a postscript written seven years after the publication of the book, Kuhn says that the critics had misconstrued his words to understand that the proponents of incommensurable theories cannot communicate with each other at all, or that theory-choice must take recourse to reasons that are ultimately personal and subjective. Kuhn clarifies that what he meant to say was quite simple. “Debates about theory-choice,” he says, “cannot be cast in a form that fully resembles logical or mathematical proof” because “that debate is about *premises*, and its recourse is to persuasion as a prelude to the possibility of proof”. Since the debate is about premises, the reasons for choice are not different than those that are frequently employed by philosophers of science: accuracy, simplicity, fruitfulness and the like. If, for example, they disagree about the relative importance of factors like simplicity and fruitfulness, neither can be convicted of a mistake. “Nor is either being unscientific,” says Kuhn. “There is no neutral algorithm for theory-choice, no systematic decision procedure which, properly applied, must lead each individual in the group to the same decision.” The reasons that ultimately decide theory-choice function as values and they can be applied differently; and the application of such values are not irrational, they just operate within the larger boundary of what may be considered as ‘reasonable’. But the choice of a theory does not necessarily yield a new theory that is more objectively true than the previous one; it is a ‘better’ theory judged only by the values, such as simplicity, fruitfulness, etc, that have acted as the drivers, over and above the better fit-to-data that it provides over the previous one, for making the decision.



Kuhn’s explanation seems reasonable. For, consider this: Would it be right to say that the Copernican heliocentric system gave us a truer description of the world than the Ptolemaic geocentric system it replaced had? Already during the time of Newton, a number of philosophers, including Leibnitz, had criticized Newton’s retention of the conception of absolute space and had hinted that a relativistic conception of space would be more appealing. Today, we agree that a system built with the sun as the frame of reference is neither true to a lesser degree nor to a greater degree than one built with the earth as the frame of reference. The Copernican system certainly provided a conceptually simpler and more elegant way to conceive the universe than the Ptolemaic system had, but simplicity and elegance cannot justifiably be considered as marks of objective truth. Again, Kuhn’s view that the shift from one paradigm to another is not entirely determined by rational factors seems to be the case. Take the case of the change from classical Newtonian physics to modern Relativistic-Quantum physics. The paradigm shift required some of the most fundamental laws of logic to be set aside in order to accommodate the uncertainty principle, which states that it is impossibility to simultaneously determine the position as well as the speed of a particle, or the dual wave—particle behaviour of electrons, which states that the nature of an electron is observer-dependent and not object-dependent. Can a theory-choice procedure which allows the fundamental laws of logic to be jettisoned be labelled as fully rational?



Kuhn’s contention that the mechanism of theory-choice is not an entirely rational process is linked to another controversial claim made by him: the claim that all data is *theory-laden*. One may recall the position of the logical positivists who held that the rationality of science derives from it being subjected to empirical tests whose objectivity we may take as assured because it appeals to neutral observational facts. But if the observational facts are themselves contaminated with theories, it would undermine the claim that science is built on rational foundations. The idea of *theory-ladenness of data* did just that. Kuhn argued that there is no such thing as neutral observational data, that all scientific data are imbued with theoretical assumptions. The ideal of theory-neutrality is an illusion, he said, for it is impossible to isolate ‘pure data’ which would be free from the theoretical presuppositions of all scientists. The failure of the logical positivists, after two decades of work, to find a way to lay down a set of objective verification criteria for scientific theories lent support to Kuhn’s theory-ladenness thesis.



In *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*, Kuhn argues that there is no unambiguous distinction between theory and data, between scientific discovery and scientific invention. “That distinction between discovery and invention,” he wrote, “or between fact and theory will.... prove to be exceedingly artificial.” Scientific discovery commences with the awareness of an anomaly in which the paradigm-induced expectations have been violated. Initially the scientist will fail to make sense of the new ‘fact’ which was the source of the awareness of anomaly. To make sense of it, he will have to await adjustments in the theory; but adjustments in theory will make the scientist see nature in a different way. In that transformed vision of nature, the ‘fact’ itself will be seen in a new way. It is only after the adjustment in theory has been made and sealed that the new ‘fact’ becomes a concrete scientific fact. Wittgenstein had said that we see nature through the webs of our theoretical constructs. If that be so, then the ‘fact’ will be distinctly ‘carved out’ from nature only when the theory has been firmed up and concretized. “Assimilating a new sort of fact demands a more than additive adjustment of theory,” wrote Kuhn, “and until that adjustment is completed – until the scientist has learnt to see nature in a different way – the new fact is not a scientific fact at all.” Kuhn provides examples from the history of science to back up his claim. In one example, he points to the difficulty we face in identifying when exactly oxygen was discovered. One may note that prior to the discovery of oxygen, the phlogiston theory was in vogue. Was oxygen discovered when the British scientist, Joseph Priestley, collected the gas released by heated red oxide of mercury as ‘common air’ with ‘less than its quantity of phlogiston’, or when Lavoisier identified it as ‘air itself entire without alteration’ except that ‘it comes out more pure’? And we may wonder what it was that Lavoisier really saw considering that even after oxygen had been accorded recognition as a standard chemical substance, he continued to insist that oxygen was an atomic ‘principle of acidity’ and that the gas was formed only when the ‘principle’ united with calorific, the matter of heat.



Many philosophers are of the opinion that Kuhn’s writings on theory-ladenness are not totally perspicuous. While the idea that perception is conditioned by background beliefs is supported by psychological experiments, the question of how much bearing it has on scientific theory-choice remains a debatable point. Likewise, while philosophers in general agree that achieving complete theory-neutrality of data may be an unattainable ideal, they remain divided on the question of the extent to which this limitation compromises the objectivity of the process of paradigm transfers. Nevertheless, no one has been able to effectively counter Kuhn’s argument that *no algorithm* for theory choice can be formulated. Kuhn’s incommensurability thesis together with his radical view that truth is paradigm-relative has also brought on him the charge that he is responsible for promoting an extreme form of relativism. But the criticism may be a bit harsh considering that Kuhn never subscribed to a hard form of relativism; he maintained the Kantian perspective that even though reality is not directly knowable, its manifestation is not determined by us and remains *resistant* to our attempts at its interpretation.



There is little doubt that Kuhn’s ideas have transformed the philosophy of science. Kuhn called into question some of the most fundamental assumptions which had until then been taken for granted and forced philosophers to confront them. Along with Duhem and Quite, he brought the question of the rationality of science to the field of open philosophical debate. And even those who opposed Kuhn had to admit that the historical study of science had much to contribute to its understanding. And Kuhn’s idea that science is intrinsically a social activity which takes place within a scientific community bound together by allegiance to a shared paradigm resonated well with most scientists and philosophers. It is not surprising therefore that Kuhn’s ideas had far reaching influence on the sociologists of science some of who went so far as to suggest that the beliefs held by scientists are in large part socially determined. While that accusation may be a bit far-fetched, Kuhn’s ideas regarding theory-ladenness and incommensurability may not be so easily dismissed. This author has shown how these ideas impinge not only on the comparisons that we make between theories belonging to two different paradigms of science but also on comparisons that we make between theories from two paradigmatically different cultures in his book ‘*Natural Realism and Contact Theory of Perception’*. The author has argued therein that the time lapse said to be ‘observed’ between the occurrence of an event in space and its perception by a human observer is a theory-laden observation (springing from the presuppositions of the stimulus-response theory of perception) and that when seen through the lens of a direct perception theory as held in the Indian tradition, no such time-lapse in observation occurs. How the prior presuppositions of our theories impinge on our observations have been illustrated in the two Annexures appended at the end of the book titled ‘*Paradigm Shift & The Incommensurability Problem: The Cases of Roemer’s and Fizeau’s Experiments’* and ‘*Paradigm Shift and the Incommensurability Problem: The Case of Perception of Lightning*’.
