Much of the initial work in the area of Philosophy of Science was a fallout of certain debates that took place among the Logical Positivists, a certain section of Analytical Philosophers who believed that clarity and exactness in philosophical thinking could be achieved only through the deployment of a precisely defined *ideal language*. In the *ideal language* as it was conceived by the Analytic philosophers, it is the sense conveyed by the sentence as a whole, and not by the individual words of which it is constituted, which contributes to its cognitive significance. That is, it has cognitive significance only when we can ask whether the sense or thought conveyed by the sentence as a whole is true or false. For example, the sentence ‘Socrates is wise’ has a sense and it has an assertoric force, that is, we can ask of it whether it is true or false. Instead of an assertoric force, we might have given the sentence an interrogative force by asking the question ‘Is Socrates wise?’ But the formulation of sentences in the reformed ideal language requires everything that has no bearing on the possible truth value of the sentence to be eliminated. Thus the new formal language, stripped of all subjective and poetic colourings and tones and constructed so as to have a single unambiguous meaning with an assertive force, distinguishes itself from ordinary language and sets itself up as a carefully crafted tool for scientific discourse.



Within the field of Analytic Philosophy, the movement known as Logical Positivism took birth from the following two fundamental ideas:



i) That only sentences of which it can be asked whether its sense is true or false are to be considered as cognitively significant / meaningful, and

ii) That the verification criterion for determining the truth of such a cognitively meaningful sentence needs to be empirical.



Based on these two ideas, the Logical Positivists - or Verificationists as they came to be called - held that metaphysical statements are not cognitively significant or meaningful; hence they held all metaphysical statements to be *non-sense*. They held science to be the only source of knowledge about the world.



The Logical Positivism movement had to be ultimately abandoned because the problem of defining the *verification criteria* for science turned out to be more problematic and intractable than it had been initially envisaged.
