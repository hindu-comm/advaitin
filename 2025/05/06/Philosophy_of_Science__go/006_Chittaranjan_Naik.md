LOGICAL POSITIVISM





Wittgenstein’s book *Tractatus Logico Philosophicus* was the central and most powerful influence on the most anti-metaphysical school of Analytic philosophy – *Logical Positivism*. Also known as *Logical Empiricism*, Logical Positivism was born in Vienna in the 1920s through the postulations of Moritz Schlick, Rudolph Carnap, Kurt Godel, Otto Neurath and others who, in turn, were influenced by the analytic and linguistic philosophies of Ernst Mach, Gottlob Frege, Bertrand Russell, Ludwig Wittgenstein and George Edward Moore. This group of philosophers, which came to be called the *Vienna Circle*, held that all propositions of metaphysics are meaningless because they cannot be verified by experience. The common ground of *Logical Positivism* was the belief that propositions are factually meaningful only if they are empirically verifiable or if they are derived out of terms that are already defined - that is, if they are tautologous. Metaphysical statements, they held, are neither verifiable nor tautologous and are hence literally meaningless. They may have emotive meaning, but not factual meaning. Since metaphysics, philosophy, ethics, religion and aesthetics are all eliminated as disciplines of factual knowledge, the only tasks of philosophy are clarification and analysis. The propositions of philosophy are linguistic, not factual, and philosophy is a department of logic.



In contrast to the schemes of thought produced by individual philosophers such as Frege, Russell and Wittgenstein, Logical Positivism was the fruit of the cooperative efforts of like-minded scientists and philosophers belonging to the Vienna Circle. The central doctrine of the Logical Positivists known as *Verificationism* may be traced to certain statements of Wittgenstein’s in his work *Philosophical Remarks* and in his conversations with the Logical Positivists



To understand the truth of a proposition means to know how the issue of its truth or falsity is to be decided.

Each proposition is a signpost for a verification.

The sense of a proposition is the method of its verification.


Apart from Wittgenstein, whose influence on the Logical Positivists was pronounced, its members also drew inspiration from the empiricism of David Hume in formulating their main doctrine of *Verificationism*. Following Hume’s lead, the Logical Positivists made a distinction between the *formal sciences* and the *empirical sciences*, as marked by the differences between *formal truths* dealt with in mathematics and logic, and *factual truths* achieved in the empirical sciences such as physics and biology. The positivists held the statements of the former to be analytic statements and those of the latter to be synthetic statements. The truths of analytic statements are known a priori; therefore the formal sciences such as logic and mathematics require no appeal to experience. With regard to synthetic statements, Kant had argued that they may be a priori or a posteriori and a large part of his philosophy was concerned with showing how synthetic a priori statements are possible. The Logical Positivists rejected the notion of synthetic a priori truths, not as false but as meaningless since the establishment of such truths requires obtaining knowledge of entities that transcends experience altogether. The *Principle of Verification* underlies these basic claims of Logical Positivism.



The Principle of Verification does not set the criterion for determining whether a statement is true or false but stipulates the condition for determining the meaningfulness of a statement, that is, its capacity to be either true or false and thereby to be a candidate for the cognitive use of language. It was used to lay down the necessary condition for inquiring into truth, namely that the propositions have meaning. The Principle of Verification thus sets the criterion for meaningfulness. Logical Positivism, as a philosophical movement, did not undertake any factual enquiry to determine which of any competing views may be true but to settle the prior question of whether the formulation of the proposition is meaningful. By adopting the Principle of Verification in this way, the Logical Positivists were able to eliminate metaphysics on the ground that its propositions, which are not verifiable by experience, are devoid of meaning. In the view of the positivists, the problems of metaphysics are spurious problems and the discipline is therefore cognitively worthless. Heidegger’s use of the term ‘Nothing’ provided much fodder for the positivists to demonstrate their claims that metaphysics is devoid of meaning. Here is a passage from Heidegger’s essay ‘*What is Metaphysics?*’ which Rudolph Carnap focuses his attention on:



“What is to be investigated is being only – *nothing else*; being alone and further – nothing; solely being, and beyond being – *nothing*. *What about this Nothing?... Does this Nothing exist only because the Not, i.e., the Negation, exists?* Or is it the other way around?.... We assert: *the Nothing is prior to the Not and the Negation*.... Where do we seek the Nothing? How do we find the Nothing... We know the Nothing.... *Anxiety reveals the Nothing*.... That for which and because of which we were anxious, was ‘really’ – nothing. Indeed: the Nothing itself – as such – was present.... *What about this Nothing? – The Nothing itself nothings*.”



Using symbolic logic, Carnap shows that even though the statements appear to possess correct grammatical forms these forms cannot even be constructed in the reformed language of logic. And among various other faults, he points out that the word ‘nothing’ has been here employed as a noun because it is customary for this form to be used in ordinary language for the purpose of constructing negative existential statements but in the correct use of language it is only a form of a certain logical structure of the sentence and not a particular name.



The Logical Positivists used the Principle of Verification not only as a destructive weapon against metaphysics but also as a tool to vindicate empirical science as the only source of knowledge about the world. But did the Principle of Verification justify the confidence placed in it by the positivists? Were the positivists able to use the principle to illuminate our understanding of the method of science? The project undertaken by them with this goal in mind almost immediately ran into difficulties, most of which were raised by the positivists themselves. A large part of the history of the Logical Positivist movement is concerned with the attempts made by the positivists to work out these difficulties, sometimes with fundamental adjustments to the principle itself.



How did the Principle of Verification do justice to the way in which we can understand, in some precise way, the actual relations between observational sensory data and the statements of advanced scientific theories? What are these relations? What are the logical connections between observation reports and the abstract mathematical formulae of scientific theories? It became necessary to look into these questions in order to be able to assert with confidence that science, unlike metaphysics, rests on the Principle of Verification. The principles enunciated in Logical Atomism provided a way to break up complex molecular propositions into simpler atomic propositions. But unlike in the formal sciences (such as logic and mathematics), the empirical sciences require that the complex propositions be broken up into simpler propositions that are ultimately empirically verifiable. For, the appeal to sensory experience is the final arbiter that determines the meaningfulness of a statement and the final basis on which its truth or falsity rests. The empirical data provided by such sensory experience is the logical foundation both for a single statement as well as for a whole web or superstructure of statements. In the terminology of the positivists, these empirical data are the empirical bases – the ‘*verifiers*’ - of a meaningful statement, and the statements that report the observations of sensory experience are basic statements or ‘*protocol statements’*. Now, the difficulties faced by the positivists may be divided into two parts: one part has to do with the questions that arise in respect of the verifiers themselves – are they established with certainty or are they open to correction? And the other has to do with the question of the logical status of the Verification Principle – does the principle not equate to a metaphysical principle and hence result in its own elimination?



To begin with, there were certain disagreements between positivists on some fundamental questions. They were divided, for example, on the question of whether there was a distinction between *hypothesis* and *proposition* as held by a group of positivists. A proposition, according to this group, is directly verified with certainty whereas a hypothesis can only be established with some degree of probability. Those who held this distinction to be valid, primarily following the lead given by Schlick and Waissman (who were in turn influenced by Wittgenstein), were known as the ‘strong verificationists’. Opposed to them were others, such as Carnap and Neurath, who came to hold the position that there was no essential difference between a hypothesis and a proposition and that all empirical statements are fundamentally hypothetical. They were the ‘weak verificationists’.



Even though the strong verificationists held that a proposition can be directly verified with certainty, they were unable to surmount the difficulty faced in establishing how a proposition can be verified by the occurrence of a single confirming experience. For, it is presupposed that the confirming experience occurs under very definite conditions; but to establish that those conditions are actually realized would require additional verifications. Also cases of illusions of sense and cases of careless observations, etc. would need to be taken into consideration. Strictly speaking, the meaning of a proposition would be exhausted only by an indefinitely large number of confirming experiences. An isolated confirming experience is therefore not truly significant. One may express this difficulty by saying that the meaning of a statement regarding the physical world requires an endless concatenation of data. One may note that the difficulty the strong verificationists faced was not with having to establish the validity of the confirming experience but with the prospect of a proposition requiring an endless chain of confirming experiences. The weak verificationists, on the other hand, denied even the possibility of primitive protocol statements, that is, of observation reports that were themselves not subject to further verification. In the words of Neurath, “*There is no way of taking conclusively established protocol statements as the starting point of the sciences*. No *tabula rasa* exists. We are like sailors who must rebuild their ship on the open sea, never able to dismantle it in dry-dock and to reconstruct it there out of the best materials.” The weak verificationists held that for a protocol statement to be complete, it must contain a proper name - the name of the person or group that confirms the experience. And such protocol statements are factual statements requiring confirmation. All of which means that there are neither primitive protocol-statements nor sentences which are not subject to further verification. It is for this reason that the weak verificationists denied the distinction between hypothesis and proposition and considered all statements regarding the physical world to be hypothetical. The end result of it all, for both the strong and the weak verificationists, is that the verification project remained open ended until the end and had to be finally abandoned.



With regard to the second problem, that is, the problem of the logical status of the Verification Principle itself, the basic difficulty is summed up by Carnap in these words:



The following objection, which on first appearance seems indeed destructive, has been repeatedly raised: - “If every proposition which does not belong either to mathematics or to the empirical investigation of facts is meaningless, how does it fare then with your own propositions? You positivists and anti-metaphysicians yourself cut off the branch on which you sit.” This objection indeed touches upon a decisive point.



Carnap tried to offer a solution to the problem by saying that the distinctive role of philosophy is to be the logic of science and that the Principle of Verifiability belongs to this department. In his book *The Logical Syntax of Language*, he tried to lay down a theory of the formal syntactical structure of the language of science. The topic of verification, he said, is to be approached in a syntactical way by ensuring syntactical coherence between the various propositions that compose the discipline. Then the solution to the problem of the verification of physical laws becomes a matter of arranging the general propositions of a certain form, representing the physical laws, and the protocol propositions to achieve syntactical deductive coherence. But this solution raised new difficulties. For, apart from ignoring important semantic questions related to empirical reference and truth, the solution offered was more in the nature of a convention to be followed than a genuine solution. The Analytic philosopher, A.J. Ayer, clearly expressed the view that the Vienna Circle adopted the Verification Principle as a convention when they made it a prescription by suggesting the kinds of statements that should be regarded as either true or false and that only statements capable of being true or false should be regarded as literally meaningful. Ayer says:



But why should this principle be accepted? The most that has been proved is that metaphysical statements do not fall into the same category as the laws of logic, or as scientific hypotheses, or as historical narratives, or judgments of perception, or any other common sense descriptions of the “natural” world. Surely it does not follow that they are neither true nor false, still less that they are nonsensical? No it does not follow.



By being reduced to the status of a convention, the Principle of Verification lost much of its initial vigour and force. It now became a mere ‘recommendation’ and lacked the destructive force required to bring about an elimination of metaphysics. The problem of finding a precise formulation of the Principle of Verification proved to be far more difficult than it had initially been foreseen by the positivists and it led eventually to the dissolution of Logical Positivism as a living philosophical movement.
