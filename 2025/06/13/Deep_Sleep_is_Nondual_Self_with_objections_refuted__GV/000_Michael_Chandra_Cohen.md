****Avasthatraya Prakriya - Suśupti (Deep Sleep)****

(from talk on: 5-9-53)

  

"Having considered the doctrine of Brahman as the cause of the universe, as established in the Brahma Sūtras, we now turn to the tre

atment of the avasthātraya prakriyā—the analysis of the three states of consciousness—within that text.

In all the Upaniṣads, it is unequivocally declared that in suṣupti (deep sleep), the jīva (individual self) becomes one with the Paramātman (Supreme Self). Nowhere in śruti do we find a teaching that avidyā (ignorance) persists in suṣupti. Rather, the consistent experience is that “nothing is seen, nothing is heard, nothing is known.” The question arises: What is the reason for this absence of cognition?

Some modern interpreters of Śaṅkara propose that a so-called bhāvāvidyā (a positive, existing ignorance) veils the jīva. But the śruti offers no support for this view. Instead, it states:  
na tu tadvitīyam asti tato'nyad vibhaktaṁ yat paśyet (Bṛh. 4.3.23)  
“There is nothing separate from the Self there—if there were, would he not see it?”

This śruti makes it clear that the absence of duality is the reason for non-perception in suṣupti, as was discussed earlier.

****The Role of Deep Sleep in the Brahma Sūtras****

Let us now examine how the Brahma Sūtras approach this topic. In the fifth adhikaraṇa of the first chapter, the sūtrakāra considers the scriptural statement:  
sadeva somyedamagra āsīt (Chāndogya Up. 6.2.1)  
“O Somya, in the beginning this was Existence alone, one without a second.”

A doubt arises: Does this “sat” (Existence) refer to Brahman or to Pradhāna, the primordial matter of the Sāṅkhya system?

According to Sāṅkhyas, Pradhāna is the root cause from which emerge successive evolutes like mahat (intellect) and ahaṅkāra (ego). But according to Vedānta, sat refers to Brahman, from whom arise the five great elements and all beings. The context of the passage supports the Vedāntic view: it speaks of sat thinking, being called the Self, and serving as the ground of liberation. If Pradhāna were meant, a different soteriology would have to be introduced—yet no such view is found in śruti.

Hence, the siddhāntin affirms that sat here is indeed Brahman. Immediately following this conclusion, the sūtra 1.1.9—svāpyayāt—is introduced.

****The Meaning of Svāpyayāt (1.1.9)****

What is the significance of this sūtra?

The term svapiti—“he sleeps”—is derived from svāpyaya, meaning “merging into one's own self.” The śruti teaches that the jīva, in deep sleep, merges into sat, the ground of Being. Since Pradhāna is inert, it cannot be regarded as the jīva’s own nature (sva), nor can one meaningfully say that the conscious jīva merges into the unconscious Pradhāna. Therefore, the only meaningful interpretation is that the jīva returns to its true nature—Brahman—in deep sleep. This merging is not a destruction but a cessation of adjunct-based distinctions.

Śaṅkara writes in this context:  
sa upādhi-dvaya-uparamē suṣuptāvasthāyām upādhi-kṛta-viśeṣābhāvāt svātmani pralīna iva iti; svaṁ hy apīto bhavatīti ucyate  
“When the two upādhis (gross and subtle bodies) cease in the state of deep sleep, and distinctions caused by adjuncts are absent, the jīva appears to be merged into its own Self. Therefore, it is said that he attains his own Self.”

Thus, in suṣupti, there is no perception not because of the presence of ignorance but because of the absence of duality.

****Refuting the Notion of Avidyā in Deep Sleep****

We must now turn to a crucial clarification regarding suṣupti (deep sleep): it has been explicitly stated that no upādhi (limiting adjunct) exists for the jīva in deep sleep. Yet, some modern commentators, deviating from both śruti and bhāṣya, argue that since avidyā (ignorance) persists as an upādhi in deep sleep, the union of jīva and Brahman cannot be accepted—and if it were accepted, they claim, it would entail connecting avidyā to Brahman itself. To support this, they point to the bhāṣya phrase svātmani pralīna iva (“as if merged in one’s own Self”) and interpret it as suggesting partial or apparent merger: “Because the projective power of avidyā has subsided, he is as if merged in Brahman; but since the veiling power remains, he is still not fully merged.”

This interpretive effort is futile. Śaṅkara, commenting on the sūtra tadabhāvo nāḍīṣu tacchruterātmani ca (3.2.7), clearly states that the jīva merges into Brahman in deep sleep. We must pay particular attention to the following passage from that bhāṣya:

api ca na kadācij jīvasya brahmaṇā sampattir nāsti svarūpasy anapāyitvāt \| svapna-jāgaritayor tūpādhi-samparka-vaśāt para-rūpāpatim ivāpekṣya tad-upaśamāt suṣupte svarūpa-aparavakṣyate \| ataś ca suṣuptāvasthāyāṁ kadācit satā sampadyate kadācin na sampadyata iti ayuktam.

“Moreover, there is never a time when the jīva does not attain union with Brahman, because his essential nature is inalienable. In the states of dream and waking, due to contact with the upādhi, the jīva appears to take on a different form. But with the cessation of upādhi in suṣupti, he attains his essential nature. Hence, it is incorrect to say that sometimes he merges with sat and sometimes does not.”

The meaning of this passage is luminous and undeniable: since the jīva’s essential nature is Brahman, and since the essential never departs, there is no moment when the jīva is not in union with Brahman. However, due to the temporary assumption of a different appearance caused by upādhis in waking and dream, the disappearance of those upādhis in suṣupti is described as the attainment of one’s own Self. Thus, to speak of “partial merger” in suṣupti is fundamentally misguided. There is no trace here of avidyā persisting in deep sleep. On the contrary, it is declared with the finality of a bell ringing: there is no upādhi in Brahman.

****A Clear Statement from Śaṅkara on the Absence of Avidyā****

Furthermore, Śaṅkara continues in the same commentary, explaining the absence of awareness in deep sleep as follows:

upādher upaśāntatvāt satā sampanno na vijānāti iti yuktam.

“Because the upādhi has subsided, he has become one with sat; hence, it is appropriate that he does not know anything.”

Where then is the scope for arguing that avidyā is the cause of non-cognition in suṣupti?

****Ajātaśatru’s Teaching and the Vedāntic Position****

Let us now turn to another sūtra (1.4.18) and its bhāṣya, which refer to the conversation in the Kauṣītaki Brāhmaṇa Upaniṣad between the brāhmaṇa Bālāki and the king Ajātaśatru. Bālāki, proposing that various puruṣas dwelling in the sun, moon, etc., are Brahman, concludes that prāṇa is the true Brahman. The king, dismissing Bālāki’s view, seeks to teach the higher Brahman.

He awakens a sleeping man and asks Bālāki, “Where was this person sleeping? From where did he return?” Bālāki has no answer. The king responds: when one sleeps dreamlessly, he abides in the Self—from which all things, including prāṇa and the world, arise.

Śaṅkara writes in his commentary:

suṣuptikāle ca pareṇa brahmaṇā jīva ekatāṁ gacchati \| parasmāc ca brahmaṇaḥ prāṇādikaṁ jagat jāyata iti vedānta maryādā.

“In the state of deep sleep, the jīva becomes one with the Supreme Brahman. From that same Brahman, prāṇa and the world originate—this is the doctrine of Vedānta.”

Here too, any notion of separation between jīva and Brahman is rejected. The division arises only due to avidyā in waking and dream. Śaṅkara further writes:

tasmād yatrāsya jīvasya niḥsaṁbodhatā-svacchatā-rūpaḥ svāpaḥ, upādhi-janita-viśeṣa-jñāna-rahitaṁ svarūpam, yataḥ taṁ svarūpam āgamanaṁ, so’tra paramātmā veditavyatayā śrāvita iti gamyate.

“In that Supreme Self, where the jīva’s sleep is of the form of pure, unknowing clarity—devoid of any knowledge shaped by upādhi—and from which he emerges, it is that Supreme Self which is pointed out in the śruti as the object of realization.”

****Refuting Modern Reinterpretations****

Modern reinterpretations of this passage are wholly unsatisfactory. Some commentators twist this clear teaching into “He has a kind of purity—as if pure, likepure like a liberated soul,” and claim that real purity is absent because laya (dissolution) and distraction remain. What is one to say to this? Even if the Supreme grants a boon, such an interpreter would refuse to bestow it!

Not only is the bhāṣya’s assertion of identity with Brahman dismissed, but Bādarāyaṇa’s own doctrinal intention—identifying the paramātman in the vedya-vākya (declarative sentence)—is also lost. This alone is cause for sorrow.

****Conclusion: The Sleeping Self Is the Supreme Self****

In support of Śaṅkara’s reading, the Brhadāraṇyaka passage cited by both sūtrakāra and bhāṣyakāra is:

ya eṣo’ntar hṛdaya ākāśaḥ saḥ śete (Brh. 2.1.17)

“He sleeps in the space within the heart.”

And Śaṅkara comments:

ākāśa-śabdena para eva sva ācyate \| tasmin sve ātmany ākāśe śete svābhāvike asaṁsārike.

“By the word ākāśa, the Supreme Self is indicated. In that Supreme, natural, and non-transmigratory Self, he sleeps.”

What clearer statement could there be?

****Resolution of Objections Concerning Deep Sleep and Liberation****

Having established that the jīva becomes one with the Supreme Self (Paramātman) in suṣupti (deep sleep) and re-emerges from the same upon awakening, the sūtra and bhāṣya raise a pertinent objection (pūrvapakṣa): If true union with Brahman occurs in deep sleep, why does liberation (mokṣa) not result automatically? Why does the jīva, like a drop of water merged in the ocean, reappear and re-enter saṃsāra?

Śaṅkara offers several responses to this question. Though we cannot explore them all here, two crucial statements from the bhāṣya on Sūtra 3.2.9 are illuminating:

iha tu vidyate vivekakāraṇaṁ karma cāvidyā ca iti vaiṣamyam  
“Here (in the case of the jīva), there exist distinguishing causes—namely karma and avidyā. A drop of water merged into the ocean has no such causes.”

This clarifies that the distinction between jīva and Brahman is due to upādhi-based factors—karma and avidyā—which are absent in the case of a water drop. Hence, the jīva continues to experience re-embodiment.

Further:

sadeva tūpādhi-samparkāt jīva ityupacaryate ityasakṛt pañcitam \| evaṁ sati yāvat ekopy upādhigatā bandhān vṛttis tāvad eka-jīva-vyavahāraḥ — sa evāyam upādhiḥ svāpa-prabodhayor bīja-aṅkura-nyāyena iti ataḥ sa eva jīvaḥ pratibudhyate iti yuktam

“It has been repeatedly explained that Brahman itself is figuratively called jīva due to its contact with the upādhi. As long as the same upādhi persists, bondage continues, and the same jīva is referred to in both deep sleep and waking, just as a seed and sprout represent two states of the same entity. Therefore, it is proper to say that the very same jīva awakens after sleep.”

****Clarifying the Role of Avidyā and Buddhi****

Because this passage mentions avidyā among the causes of reappearance, some interpreters have erroneously concluded that avidyā continues in suṣupti. But since Śaṅkara mentions both karma and avidyā and considering that karma clearly does not operate in deep sleep, it is improper to assume that avidyā exists there in the same active sense. The sentence is metaphorical, pointing to the continuity of the upādhi, not its activity.

The natural question then arises: If avidyā is not the upādhi that persists, what is?

Śaṅkara answers directly:

paramārthatas tu na jīvo nāma buddhy-upādhi-sambandha-parikalpita-svarūpa-vyatirekeṇa asti  
“From the standpoint of the highest truth, there is no such thing as a jīva apart from the Paramātman; it is merely a construct based on association with the buddhi (intellect) as upādhi.”

And how does this association arise?

mithyājñāna-puraḥsaraḥ ayam ātmanaḥ buddhy-upādhi-sambandhaḥ  
“This relationship with the buddhi arises from false knowledge (mithyājñāna).”

This false association, existing in seed form even during suṣupti and cosmic dissolution (pralaya), manifests again upon waking:

ayam api buddhi-sambandhaḥ śakty-ātmanā vidyamāna eva suṣupta-pralayayoḥ punaḥ prabodha-prasavayoḥ āvirbhavati  
“This connection with buddhi, though latent in deep sleep and dissolution, reappears upon waking.”

Thus, in suṣupti there is no experience of the world; in jāgrat (waking), the world with its differentiated objects reappears—its source being avidyā. If this avidyā were intrinsic to the Self, it could never be eliminated. The truth is that the Self is untouched by the three states (avasthātraya) and by avidyā.

To express this insight, Śrī Gauḍapāda declares in his Kārikā:

anādimāyayā supto yadā jīvaḥ prabudhyate \|  
ajam anidram asvapnam advaitaṁ budhyate tadā (Gau. Kā. 1.16)

“This jīva, asleep under beginningless māyā, upon awakening through true knowledge, realizes himself as unborn (aja), sleepless (anidra), without dreams (asvapna), and non-dual (advaita).”

****The Nature of Union (Sampatti) in Suṣupti****

To complete the analysis of suṣupti, we cite the Chāndogya Śruti:

satā somya tadā sampanno bhavati (Ch. 6.8.1)  
“O Somya, in that state, he becomes one with sat (Being).”

What is the nature of this sampatti (attainment)? It is not samyoga-sambandha (conjunction, as when one object newly joins another), nor is it samavāya-sambandha (inherent relation, as between substance and quality). Śaṅkara clarifies this in the bhāṣya on Sūtra 3.2.34:

buddhy-upādhi-sthāna-viśeṣa-yogād udayasya viśeṣa-vijñānasya upādhi-upaśame ya upaśamaḥ sa paramātmanā sambandhaḥ iti upādhi-apekṣayā upacaryate, na parimita-tvāpēkṣayā  
  

“Due to the upādhi of the buddhi, differentiated knowledge arises. When that upādhi subsides, the cessation itself is figuratively called a ‘relation’ with the Paramātman, not because the jīva is finite, but merely for the sake of instruction.”

Thus, Śaṅkara states that all upādhis subside in suṣupti, and the union with the Paramātman is not a real change or a new event, but a recognition of what already is.

And yet, some interpreters continue to insist that the avidyā-upādhi persists in deep sleep—establishing a novel doctrine contrary to the bhāṣya. Those who seek truth must reflect on this carefully.

Even when the term upaśama (cessation) is used, some argue that the upādhi is not fully dissolved but only temporarily dormant (unmanifest). Since the relationship with the upādhi arises from mithyājñāna, they hold that it remains until true knowledge (jñāna) dawns. This may be granted.

But the crucial question remains: Is the jīva ultimately distinct from the Paramātman?

****On the Absence of Upādhi in Deep Sleep: Clarifying the Bhāṣya****

To contemplate thus is to prepare the mind for Self-knowledge (ātma-jñāna). Therefore, even if avidyā appears to exist in suṣupti (deep sleep) from the deluded standpoint of the worldly intellect, it does not exist from the standpoint of śruti (scripture). In fact, while examining suṣupti, it is never appropriate to assert the continued presence of an upādhi or avidyā. To emphasize this, the commentator clearly states in the next sūtra:

“svam apīto bhavati iti svarūpa-sambaddham enam āmananti \| svarūpasya cānapāyitvāt na nara-nagara-nyāyena sambandho bhavati \|
upādhi-kṛta-svarūpa-tirobhāvāt tu svam apīto bhavati ity upapadyate”  
  

“The statement 'he merges into his own Self' is an affirmation of the Self's intrinsic nature. Since that nature is never lost, the relationship is not like a man walking into a city. Rather, the sense of merging arises because the upādhi-caused obscuration of the true nature disappears.”

****Deep Sleep and Samādhi: No Essential Difference****

According to the Brahmasūtra Bhāṣya, the conclusion regarding suṣupti is that there is no upādhi present there, and the jīva truly becomes one with Brahman. Therefore, there is no essential difference between suṣupti and samādhi; in both states, from the standpoint of ultimate reality (paramārthika satya), the jīva abides in unity with the Paramātman.

From the standpoint of the ignorant, however, avidyā is projected as if it persists. Some interpret Gaudapāda as distinguishing suṣupti from samādhi, thereby implying a doctrinal difference between him and Śaṅkara. But this is a misunderstanding. Their views are in perfect agreement. The perceived divergence arises not from their teachings but from our own misapprehension.

Numerous bhāṣya passages across the Prasthāna-traya affirm the unity of the Sūtra and Upaniṣad traditions concerning suṣupti. For one familiar with the method of the bhāṣya, this harmony is self-evident. But for those less accustomed to its study, doubts may arise: “Can everyone with discriminative power recognize whether Śaṅkara’s doctrine is unanimously supported here or not?”

****No Real Relation Between Self and Non-Self****

The apparent relation between the Self (ātman), the witness (sākṣin), and the non-Self—intellect, senses, body—is not a real relation. This is in full accord with our actual experience. Moreover, the absence of upādhi in suṣupti is not merely scriptural—it is experientially verifiable. Everyone who sincerely contemplates in accordance with experience (anubhava-anusandhāna) will arrive at the non-dual conclusion that the Self alone is real.

But it is a mistake to claim that we become Brahman in suṣupti. Animals, birds, and even insects experience suṣupti, yet they do not attain liberation through it. Why? Because Brahmatva (the nature of Brahman) is not something that must be acquired—it is our eternal nature. As the avadhūta-s declare:

“If you say ‘Fire!’ I will enter—  
You are Brahman even now.”footnote, please

But merely being Brahman does not grant fulfillment. Liberation is attained only through the clear and certain knowledge (jñāna) that “I am the non-dual Self.”

****The Distinction Between Sleep and Knowledge****

We are fulfilled by the knowledge that “I am the non-dual Self,” not by suṣupti alone. This is why Gauḍapāda asserts a difference between the state of deep sleep and the state of realization. Madmen awaken from suṣupti still mad. But when the knowledge arises—“I am Brahman, untouched by the three states”—then, regardless of age, gender, or education, one becomes a jñānī.

The belief that “I possess states” is itself avidyā, a dream-like sleep. But the direct insight, “I am the eternal, stainless Self, without any state,” is vidyā-samādhi. Through that vidyā, one realizes the Self as ānanda-svarūpa—eternal bliss itself.

To reiterate: the supposed relation between the Self and non-Self adjuncts like buddhi (intellect), body, or senses is not a real relation. This conforms not only to scriptural teaching but also to a well-considered analysis of our own experience."
