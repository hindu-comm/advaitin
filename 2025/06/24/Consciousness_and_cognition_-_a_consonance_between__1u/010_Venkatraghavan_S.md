Namaste Putran ji,

  

Re:

> Can you also explain why sometimes jnanam is said to be a pointer to
> Brahman whereas other times it is almost equated to Brahman? In our
> experience, Existence, Consciousness, Bliss seem to refer to three
> distinct facets of manifestation; so when they are all used to denote
> nirguna Brahman, we have to think of them as equivalent pointers to
> the Reality whose primary manifest appearance/cognition involves these
> three aspects. Yet in other places, they may seem to be taken as
> practically synonymous to Brahman or its fundamentalnature. Is such
> latter usage an arthavada? If not, and we say in one place "Brahman is
> Existence" and in another "Brahman is Consciousness" and in a third
> "Brahman is Ananda", that would seem to contradict the non-duality -
> because again, in vyavaharika, those words have different meanings.

These are pretty profound questions which get to the heart of the matter. I will try to answer to the best of my knowledge - others may offer their perspectives as well.

  

First things first, we should differentiate the words "satyam", "jnAnam" and "anantam" from the entities that they denote. That is, the shabda and its artha are different. How the word denotes its meaning can be directly or indirectly. When it comes to Brahman, all words are indirect pointers, they cannot be direct denotations, whereas the meaning of the words *is* the entity itself. Therefore, when sometimes people refer to jnAnam as a pointer to Brahman, I believe they may be referring to the word "jnAnam". If they refer to the entity itself, then the entity jnAnam is not a pointer to Brahman. It is Brahman.

  

This brings us to your second question. Is the meaning of the words "satyam", "jnAnam" and "anantam" in the context of "satyam jnAnam anantam brahma" a facet of Brahman or is it Brahman itself? The answer Vedanta gives is that the meaning of satyam is jnAnam, is anantam, is brahma. It is one undivided whole.

  

You say that this appears to contradict non-duality - I will attempt to articulate why I don't think it to be so.

  

Take the case of fire - the conventional thinking is that light and heat are properties of fire. Let us refine this example further and assume momentarily that there is no such thing as light, and no such thing as heat, apart from this fire (i.e. apart from fire, light or heat are nowhere else to be found). Let us also momentarily assume that this is the only instance of fire.

  

The conclusion that we arrive at is a) light itself is fire; b) heat is fire; c) light is heat; d) and apart from light and heat, there is no such thing as fire. That is, there is nothing to separate light from heat or heat from light, or the two from fire. Further, if this happens to be the only single instance of fire in the entire universe, there is no gradation of heat or of light to measure the "extent" of this fire's heat and light vs some other fire's.

  

We may consider heat and fire to be different manifestations of the one fire - but they are not; they are fire itself - we consider them to be different, n*ot because they are different, but because we experience them differently*. There is a different sense organ for the experience of heat (touch) and for light (vision), so we consider these to be properties of fire, rather than fire in its essence.

  

Coming to satyam, jnAnam, anantam - this is something similar. Vedanta postulates that the only source of satyam (existence), jnAnam (consciousness), anantam (infiniteness) is Brahman. There is nothing else which is existent, or conscious or infinite. So what differentiates satyam, from jnAnam from anantam? It is merely our experience of each, which is different. We experience the existence of a pot differently from the cognition of a flower, so we treat knowledge as something different to existence. But like in the case of fire and heat, the difference lies not in existence and consciousness, rather the difference lies in our medium of experience. We "see" the existence of a pot, and so we understand it to be a "thing". We know that we know, and so we think of consciousness as different to existence. We "feel" the happiness differently from how we "see" existence and think of the two to be different. We marvel at the vastness of the universe and guess its infinitude. In each case, existence, consciousness, bliss, infinitude are experienced to various degrees, differently. Thus we conclude that existence is different to consciousness, which is different to bliss, which is different to infinitude. We think of these as different because we experience them differently.

  

With that as a background, when we hear the shruti then saying "satyam jnAnam anantam brahma" or "Anandam brahmeti vyajAnAt" we think of these as manifestations of Brahma. They are not - they are the same one, undivided entity. We bring our history of experiences with our multitudinous cognitive faculties to the shruti's statement of that one entity, and think of these as different aspects of that entity.

  

Which is why when analysing what is existence in the Advaita Siddhi, the author Madhusudana Sarasvati svAmi considers various definitions of existence and finding flaws in each such definition, concludes that the only thing we can know about existence is that it is self-evident consciousness. I don't intend to bring that into the discussion here, because it is very technical and one may miss the forest for the trees - I am only presenting that conclusion and trying to show how that conclusion is in consonance with our experience.

  

Looking at our own experience - When we observe the existence of objects in the world, the certainty of the knowledge of the objects' existence can never match the certainty of the knowledge of my - the subject's - existence. I know that I exist, but even when seeing a pot, I do not have the same degree of certainty of the pot's existence that I have of my own. I experience my existence as a lived-in reality - I merely observe the existence of the pot.

  

Further, when analysing my my own existence, I ultimately end up not being able to distinguish my existence from my awareness of my existence. That is, the one absolute certainty that I possess is my own existence, and even here, I am unable to distinguish my existence from my consciousness. If I cannot distinguish my own existence from my own consciousness, something that I am absolutely certain about, can I say with any certainty that existence is different to consciousness?

  

I will stop here for the moment, as I think we have got pretty deep and going further may just end up confusing.
