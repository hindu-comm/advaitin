This passage attempts to argue that all four classical types of abhāva (absence) — prāgabhāva (prior absence), dhvaṁsābhāva (destructional absence), atyantābhāva (absolute non-existence), and anyonyābhāva (mutual non-existence) — are not truly "absences", but are better understood as bhāvarūpa (positive entities), since we describe them in terms of something else that is.

It uses the example of a pot’s absence being a cloth’s presence, and concludes that no kind of absence is ever truly devoid of being.

Now let’s analyze this from the standpoint of Abhāvarūpa Avidyā-vāda (as upheld by Satchidānandendra Saraswati, with close fidelity to Śaṅkara’s method).

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 🔍 ABHĀVARŪPA RESPONSE

### 1. Category Mistake Between Logical Negation and Ontological Reality

The central flaw in the above argument is that it confuses the linguistic or conceptual dependence of absence on some referent ("pot") with ontological existence.

Just because “absence of pot” is spoken of with reference to something else (like “there is no pot on the cloth”), it does not follow that absence is a thing or that the cloth is the positive substitute for the pot's absence.

From the abhāvarūpa view, abhāva is purely conceptual or epistemic:

- It does not have an existence of its own.  
    

- It is not a thing.  
    

- It is not an effect.  
    

- It is simply a negation—a lack, and not a substance or event.  
    

### 2. Vyavahāra Allows Talk of Abhāva Without Reifying It

In ordinary discourse (vyavahāra), we certainly speak of:

- “The absence of a pot here,”  
    

- “Darkness in the room,”  
    

- “Not knowing the answer,”  
    

But none of these require that the absence is a positive being.

Abhāva is a pratyakṣa or anupalabdhi-based cognition:

It is not a bhāva (a real existent), but a conceptual marker of something that is not present — yet without any positive ontological standing.

So when the passage says:

"The absence of the pot is the cloth",  
this only holds linguistically, not ontologically. The cloth is not the absence of the pot. It is simply what is present where the pot is absent. The absence itself has no being.

### 3. Applies Equally to Jñāna-Abhāva

If someone tries to say:

"Just as the absence of the pot is a cloth, the absence of knowledge (jñāna-abhāva) is a positive thing, i.e., avidyā is bhāvarūpa,"

then abhāvarūpa-vāda replies:

- No. The absence of knowledge is just that — an absence.  
    

- What follows from that absence may be misapprehension (e.g., snake in
  rope), but avidyā itself is not a bhāva.  
    

- Śaṅkara’s method is clear: avidyā is not a "thing" to be destroyed,
  but an error to be seen through.  
    

- It vanishes the moment knowledge arises — which no real entity does.  
    

### 4. Conceptual Reference ≠ Positive Reality

The deeper philosophical mistake here is thinking:

"If something is referable or describable, it must be a positive entity."

But from Śaṅkara’s standpoint:

- Many things are referable in language or thought but are not real.  
    

- Adhyāsa, mithyā-jñāna, dreams, and avidyā itself are cognitive
  constructs, not real entities.  
    

- The classic examples of silver in nacre, snake in rope, and dreams all
  support this non-entity status.  
    

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 🪷 CONCLUSION (Abhāvarūpa View)

This passage wrongly infers ontological being from linguistic or functional usage. It reifies absence into substance, conflating conceptual absence with material presence.

In Śaṅkara's teaching, and especially as clarified by Satchidānandendra Saraswati, abhāva is not bhāva — neither in pots, nor in knowledge.  
Avidyā is jñānābhāva (absence of knowledge) — not a real force, not a bhāvarūpa veil, and certainly not a substitute cognition.
