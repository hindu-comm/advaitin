Bhagavan Ramana Maharshi is a very clear modern example of a jivanmukta who realized the Self without any prior formal Vedantic study or even a physical guru, and only later found that his experience was perfectly corroborated by the teachings of Vedanta.

  

Per my understanding, there are multiple useful levels from which to look at your question.

  

**1. From the standpoint of the Self and Vedanta as pramana**

**  **

As others have mentioned, who you truly are - the Self - is ever present and never absent. Vedanta does not create the Self or produce enlightenment as a new event. It functions as a pramana, a means of knowledge, that turns the mind inward, removes ignorance, and allows what is always present to be clearly recognized.

  

In traditional language, Vedanta helps the prepared mind:

- Turn inward and discriminate through the layers (koshas)
- Resolve confusion about what is “I” and what is “not-I”
- Culminate in the rise of the one, non-dual knowledge (akhandakara
  vritti) that sublates ignorance

  

That is the whole purpose of Vedanta: to remove self-ignorance. When that ignorance is removed, the ever-shining Self is unobscured, like clouds dispersing to reveal the sun that was never absent.

  

Now, must this always happen through explicit study of Upanishads, Gita, Brahma Sutras, etc., with a formal guru? Advaita Siddhanta would say: the Self is not bound by any particular pedagogy. It is entirely possible that, owing to powerful purva-janma samskaras and Ishvara’s grace, there can be a spontaneous, intense inner turning that:

- Introverts the mind
- Cuts through identification with body, mind, and koshas
- Culminates in the same non-dual recognition that formal Vedantic study
  aims to generate

  

In that sense, the structure of what happens is Vedantic, even if the person has never heard the word “Vedanta.” Bhagavan Ramana Maharshi’s death-experience in his teens is exactly of this type. The later encounter with Vedanta did not “produce” his realization, but gave him a language, conceptual clarity, and a lineage in which that realization could be taught and communicated to others.

  

Even in the Upanishads themselves, many of the dialogues are between guru and disciple at a time when there is no “prior Upanishad syllabus” to study. The Upanishads record first-generation students who gain knowledge directly from a living guru, not from a library of pre-existing texts. ForBhagavan Ramana Maharshi, one can say Ishvara as the inner Self functioned as the guru.

  

So, to your question “Would traditional Advaitins refuse to acknowledge a jivanmukta solely because he has not studied the scriptures?” -in principle, no. The criterion is not the outer form of study, but the presence of stable, non-dual knowledge, freedom from doership/ownership, and the absence of binding desires and fears. But they would interpret such a realization as still being in keeping with the vision of Vedanta, and they would cross-check it against that vision.

  

**2. Adhikari categories and rare “spontaneous” realization**

**  **

Traditional Advaita does recognize differing levels of preparedness (adhikara) based on how thoroughly sadhana-chatushtaya (viveka, vairagya, shatka-sampatti, mumukshutva) has been assimilated:

- Manda adhikari – relatively unprepared; needs long preparation,
  karma-yoga, upasana for substantial chitta-shuddhi (mental
  purification) and chitta-ekagrata (mental focus).
- Madhyama adhikari – moderately prepared; still requires repeated
  shravana–manana–nididhyasana.
- Uttama adhikari – highly prepared; for such a person, one clear
  teaching from the guru can be enough for the akhandakara vritti to
  arise.

  

Students in the Upanishadic dialogues are generally presented as uttama adhikaris: a few statements from the guru, some sharp manana, and knowledge is clear and firm.

  

Advaita Siddhanta also implicitly acknowledge an even rarer type, which we might call parama-uttama: in them, the culmination of past samskaras and Isvara's grace is so powerful that an intense inner crisis or inquiry becomes its own “guru-word”, and the mind resolves into Self-knowledge without any formal instruction. Bhagavan Ramana Maharshi is usually placed in this category. Others often cited in this spirit include Bhagavan Shuka Maharshi, Swami Hastamalakacharya, and Swami Nammalvar. One could also make a case, from a broader perspective, for someone like Gautama Buddha as an “extra-systemic” example of such maturity and insight.

  

These cases are not used to downgrade Vedanta. They are used to show that the normative path is guru–shastra–sampradaya, and that this norm is there precisely because most of us are manda or madhyama adhikaris.The ultra-rare exceptions are acknowledged but not held out as a model for the average seeker to bypass systematic study.

  

So, when you say:

  

“Vedanta primarily aids in recognizing enlightenment by offering conceptual clarity and language, and thereby the continuous practices of shravana, manana and nidhidhyasana to achieve the realization. While realization may also arise through alternative or undocumented paths…”

  

I would nuance it slightly in traditional terms:

- Vedanta is not only “conceptual clarity and language”; it is regarded
  as the final pramana for Brahman, the direct means for removing
  ignorance.
- Life experiences, austerities, and non-Vedantic disciplines can
  greatly mature the mind - dissolve desires, reduce ego, fear, and
  expectation - and thereby make one a high-grade adhikari.
- For such an adhikari, the final, immediate recognition “there is no
  bondage, therefore no liberation is needed” can come through a single
  Vedantic statement, through the presence of a realized person, or even
  as a spontaneous inner resolution.

  

Traditional Advaita would still interpret the content of that recognition as exactly what Vedanta reveals, whether or not the person uses Vedantic terminology.

  

**3. What only Vedanta uniquely reveals: Brahman and the jiva-jagat-Ishvara relationship**

**  **

Here it is helpful to distinguish two levels very clearly:

  

1.  Realization of the true nature of the Self: as ever-present,
    non-objectifiable awareness, free from birth and death, not limited
    by body or mind. In rare cases, this can arise without consciously
    taking Vedanta as pramana in this lifetime. A person may be inwardly
    free of fear and doership and know directly “I am not this perishing
    body–mind.” 2.  Precise knowledge that “this very Self is 'Brahman', one without a
    second,” and the clear understanding of how the individual (jiva),
    the world (jagat), and Ishvara are related to this Brahman.

  

This second piece is not available to the senses, nor to unaided reasoning. It is revealed only by shastra, that is, Vedanta as pramana. Equations such as “ayam atma brahma” and the detailed Advaitic vision that jiva–jagat–Ishvara are mithya while Brahman alone is satya do not arise merely by private experience; they are taught and unfolded by the tradition.

  

So we can say:

- Realization of the Self can, in principle, occur without formal
  exposure to Vedantic texts.
- Knowing that “this Self is the non-dual Brahman, the truth of jiva,
  jagat, and Ishvara,” in a structured and communicable way, comes only
  from Vedanta.

  

This distinction becomes critical when we speak of a tattva-darshi jivanmukta jnani who is also a guru. For a liberated person who lives quietly, personal realization may be enough. For one who teaches:

- Their insight must be in full harmony with the Vedantic vision of
  Brahman, not just a private formulation.
- They need shastra-aligned clarity about the relationship between jiva,
  jagat, and Ishvara and Brahman, so they can resolve students’ doubts
  consistently and avoid leading them into partial or self-contradictory
  views.
- Their teaching must stand as a continuation of the sampradaya, not as
  an isolated testimony that cannot be tested against a shared pramana.

  

This is exactly what we see with Bhagavan Ramana Maharshi. The realization is spontaneous, not text-produced. Yet when he later encounters Vedantic works, he affirms that they describe his experience precisely. From that point, his pointers, teachings and guidance to others are entirely aligned with Advaita Vedanta, even when he uses very simple language. In that sense, Vedanta provides the universal framework and vocabulary within which a tattva-darshi jivanmukta can reliably teach others.

**4. Does Vedanta warn against clinging to words and constructs?**

**  **

Yes, very much so. The tradition repeatedly cautions against mistaking terminology and intellectual constructs for the reality they point to:

  

- Mundaka Upanishad distinguishes between apara vidya (all Vedas,
  Vedangas, etc.) and para vidya as that by which the imperishable is
  known. In other words, scriptural learning as information is “lower”;
  the direct recognition of Brahman is “higher”.
- Kena and Taittiriya Upanishadsemphasize that Brahman cannot be
  objectified by speech or mind, even though Vedanta still uses speech
  as a provisional pointer.
- Texts like Bhaja Govindam explicitly mock mere grammatical and
  intellectual obsession that does not lead to release from samsara.

  

So it is fully in line with Vedanta to say: do not cling rigidly to the terminology or intellectual constructs, or to the idea that “my particular conceptual map is the Truth.” The constructs are upayas that are meant to be outgrown once their work is done.

**  **

**5. Does Advaita encourage openness to realization outside formal Vedanta?**

  

Within its own framework, Advaita is both:

- Strict about the nature of realization: it must be clear, non-dual
  Self-knowledge (akhandakara vritti) that ends the sense of bondage and
  doership, not a fleeting experience or mystical high.
- Open about the outer form in which this realization may flower: it
  need not be preceded by a modern “course” in Vedanta or even explicit
  textual study in this life.

  

So, yes, I would say Advaita does encourage a guarded openness to such possibilities. It warns us not to absolutize pedagogy. At the same time, it warns seekers not to bypass the tried-and-tested means of guru, shastra, shravana-manana-nididhyasana by idealizing rare exceptions.

  

From the side of the jivanmukta, statements such as “there is no bondage, so no liberation is needed” are perfectly valid. From the side of the sadhaka, they can become dangerous slogans if not grounded in genuine self-knowledge. Traditional Advaita holds both these standpoints together.
