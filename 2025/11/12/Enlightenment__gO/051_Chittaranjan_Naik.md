Dear Sri Dennis-ji,

  

I don't find much to disagree with in what you say. I too hold that you don't need to know all those technical terms used in the post-Shankara Advaita texts or the arguments contained in them in order to attain an intellectual understanding of Advaita. That is the reason I had averred in one of my earlier posts that a sadhaka needs only that much from these texts as would help him in removing his doubts and the rest may be ignored.

  

I think the various Advaita texts that have sprung up after Shankara serve a different purpose than addressing just individual needs. This is because of the nature of the Vedic tradition itself. A Siddhanta, by definition, is an established tenet, so it becomes the responsibility of the tradition to ensure that its truths and principles are always maintained in a state of being an 'established' Siddhanta thereby ensuring that the authority of its utterances as valid Verbal Testimony is not destroyed. Unfortunately, as pointed out by you, pursuing such a goal has resulted in a plethora of technical terms and compex arguments which can deter ordinary aspirants from approacing Advaita and can cause confusion in the mind of a seeker who doesn't know how to navigate its complex terrain.

 

Having said all this, I think the distinction between mediate knowledge and immediate knowledge cannot be set aside so easily because it serves an important purpose in understanding Advaita itself inasmuch as it points to the fact that an intellectual understanding of Advaita does not constitute a complete understanding of Advaita. The realization of the Advaitic Truth is not merely a realization of an intellectual understanding of its truth but a culmination of the process of knowing of that truth that goes beyond the intellect to its consummation in direct cognition. Let me elaborate a bit on this...

  

In the case of the acquisition of other mediate knowledge, such as the knowledge of the presence of fire obtained from inferring it from the observation of smoke, it is to be noted that fire is an object that we are familiar with on account of it having been known beforehand through perception. Indeed, the vyapti of the inferential scheme does not operate unless both the fire (sadya) and the smoke (hetu) have been known beforehand through perception. So, a vaild mediate knowledge of an object is obtained only when it has been preceded by an immediate knowledge of that object through perception. But in the case of an intellectual understanding of Brahman, it is a kind of mediate knowledge that has never been preceded by any mode of cognition by which it could have been known immediately. Hence that mediate intellectual knowledge of Brahman cannot be considered as a complete or a fully genuine knowledge of Brahman. This is what is meant by saints like Sri Ramakrishna Paramahamsa when they point out that you may argue endlessly about the taste of a mango but unless you have tasted it yourself, you will never know really what its taste is. So, while on the one hand the words of a mere Brahmanishta cannot be taken as fully reliable, on the other hand the words of a mere Shrotriya may end up straightjacking Advaita, not being sufficiently mindful of the fact that any articulation of Advaita is constrained to be inherently paradoxical.

  

Warm regards,

Chittaranjan
