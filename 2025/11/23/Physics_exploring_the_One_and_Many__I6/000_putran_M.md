A new study explores a startling mathematical outcome that arises when physicists apply quantum-gravity and holographic ideas to a closed universe: they find that such a universe can, or appears to, have literally one single quantum state, meaning its entire quantum description would require no bits of information.  
  
That conclusion conflicts sharply with the richness of the world we inhabit, with stars, black holes, life and observers. The paradox emerges from attempts to describe a universe entirely “from the outside,” without referencing any observer inside it.  
  
In work building on the holographic principle and the “island formula” (initially developed to address black-hole information puzzles), researchers such as Juan Maldacena, Ying Zhao and others showed that a closed, boundary-less universe in these models leads to a trivial Hilbert space with just one dimension, no information to encode variation.  
  
And yet our universe clearly exhibits enormous variation and complexity. The suggested resolution is that you cannot treat the universe as if it contains no observer; the act of observation (or the presence of an observer) changes the quantum framework.  
  
When you include the observer (or treat them as a kind of boundary), the Hilbert space enlarges and complexity returns. This implies a profound shift: instead of seeking a “view from nowhere,” physics may require a framework inherently tied to observers or their boundaries.  
  
In other words, the complexity we see may depend not just on physical systems, but on who or what is observing. The authors emphasize that this is far from a final story, but it raises the possibility that a universe without observers is not only uninteresting, but even “empty” in the quantum-information sense.
