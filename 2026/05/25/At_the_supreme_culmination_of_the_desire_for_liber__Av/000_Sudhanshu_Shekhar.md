In the Vedānta texts it is found everywhere that four disciplines are to be accomplished by seekers of liberation. Among these, discrimination, dispassion, the sixfold wealth beginning with śama, and desire for liberation — these constitute the fourfold discipline.

  

The ascertainment “Brahman is real, the world is mithyā” is expressed by the word \*viveka\* (discrimination). Here it is to be understood that in the discrimination between the eternal and the non-eternal, the word “non-eternal” is indicative of mithyātva (illusoriness). Since foolish people desire impermanent happiness, whereas the illusory happiness seen in dream is not desired, therefore in Vedānta it is not intended merely that Brahman is eternal and the world non-eternal; rather, what is taught is indeed “Brahman is real, the world is mithyā.” From this very discrimination dispassion arises. One should not be deluded here into thinking that no result arises merely from the understanding of the impermanence of the visible world. Even from that understanding dispassion does arise; however, unless there is ascertainment of the mithyātva of the visible world, that dispassion does not attain firmness. Therefore ascertainment of the mithyātva of the world must indeed be accomplished.

  

Dispassion (\*vairāgya\*) is revulsion toward objects pertaining to this world and the next. All these objects are impermanent and mithyā in nature. They are entirely to be neglected and abandoned. “May no relation whatsoever arise between me and these; may the cognition of reality with respect to these never arise in me” — the disposition born of such revulsion is called dispassion.

  

Now, among the sixfold wealth beginning with śama: śama is the changeless and steady abiding of the mind, following dispassion, in its own goal, namely Brahman, whether qualified or unqualified. This śama is born of dispassion. That dispassion is born of discrimination has already been stated earlier. Repeated seeing of defects in sense-objects also plays a great role in the attainment of dispassion. The intent is that by whatever means dispassion may arise, it must certainly be accomplished. From that dispassion śama arises.

  

The turning away of the senses from the dangerous paths constituted by objects such as sound and the establishing of them in their own spheres is called \*dama\*.

  

Thus, when the senses have been properly restrained, the mind abandons transformations into the forms of objects and attains steady abidance. That state is designated by the term \*uparati\*.

  

The endurance of all sufferings without anxiety, lamentation, or attempts at counteraction is called \*titikṣā\*. In this titikṣā all the preceding disciplines such as discrimination, dispassion, śama, dama, and uparati assist.

  

The cognition of truth endowed with firm conviction regarding the scriptures declaring “Tat tvam asi” and regarding the words of the teacher conforming thereto is denoted by the word \*śraddhā\*. Through that śraddhā a person proceeds upon the path of discipline conforming to Vedānta.

  

Now, it was said that by the word \*śama\* is meant the changeless abiding of the mind, whose nature consists in alternatives and counter-alternatives, in its own goal. At present, by the word \*samādhāna\* is meant the proper and constant fixing of the determinative intellect in pure Brahman devoid of limiting adjuncts.

  

Thus śama, dama, uparati, titikṣā, śraddhā, and samādhāna — this constitutes the sixfold wealth, the third discipline among the fourfold discipline.

  

Now desire for liberation (\*mumukṣutva\*) is to be examined. Whatever object, beginning from egoity, has been grasped by us as the Self — all that binds us with a firm noose. This bondage, being an object of perception, is mithyā; and being mithyā, it is born of ignorance of the Self. Liberation from this bondage is therefore possible only through knowledge of the Self, since ignorance is removable only by knowledge. The desire to be freed by Self-knowledge from bondage produced by ignorance of the Self is designated by the term \*mumukṣutā\*. This mumukṣutā is threefold: mild, middling, and intense. Only when, among the fourfold disciplines, the second and fourth — namely dispassion and desire for liberation — are intense, do śama and the others become meaningful and fruitful. The intention is that śama and the others become meaningful only when dispassion is intense, and become fruitful only when desire for liberation is intense.

  

All this is explained in about ten verses beginning from the twentieth verse of Vivekacūḍāmaṇi. The commentary composed by Chandrasekhara Bharati Swamigal should also be consulted here.

  

Now something here deserves reflection. In the fourfold discipline, each preceding discipline functions as the cause of the succeeding discipline. Thus it is clear that discrimination, dispassion, śama, and the rest are all causes with respect to desire for liberation. Here it is to be understood that what is intended is intense desire for liberation. But when, through the highest culmination of discrimination, dispassion, śama, and the rest, desire for liberation attains intensity; when the suffocating nature of bondage is clearly seen; when our bondage and our wretchedness are perceived as clearly as an āmalaka fruit in the hand — then for the first time a true effort to become free from bondage is undertaken by us. Then for the first time the importance of Self-knowledge is seen. “What does my teacher say? What is declared by the scriptures? That my bondage is mithyā! That my identification of the Self with ego, intellect, and body is mithyā!” Then, “I am not any of these visible things; nor indeed do I have any relation whatsoever with these visible entities.” Further, “If indeed my bondage itself does not exist, then for what purpose and from what should I strive for liberation?”

  

All this visible world is to be neglected. Neither bondage nor liberation belongs to me. Before me, visible entities unrelated to me proceed on. Let them indeed proceed. From that there is neither any loss nor any gain to me.

  

Thus even desire for liberation is abandoned.

  

This very principle can also be known from the Kaṭha Upaniṣad. There, in the twenty-fourth mantra of the second vallī of the first chapter, scripture declares: “This Self is not attained by one who has not desisted from bad conduct, nor by one who is unrestrained, nor by one who is uncollected, nor even by one whose mind is unpeaceful.” Here it is explained by what kind of person the Self cannot be attained. This Self is not attained by one who has not withdrawn from sinful actions. Nor is this Self attained by a person whose mind is unpeaceful through attachment to the senses. Likewise, the Self is not attained by one who is not collected — that is, who lacks one-pointedness and possesses a distracted mind. Moreover, the Self is not attained even by one whose mind is unpeaceful. Explaining the term “unpeaceful-minded,” the commentator says: “Even one whose mind is collected, if he desires the fruit of samādhāna, is indeed unpeaceful-minded and mentally occupied.” The intent here is this: even a person of collected mind, if he desires the fruit of samādhāna, cannot attain the Self. A person desiring the fruit of samādhāna cannot become peaceful-minded. Because of the absence of peace in the mind, abiding in the nature of the Self is impossible indeed. Therefore even the desire for freedom from bondage must be abandoned. Whatever desire may arise, that indeed must be abandoned. Hence the intention is that at the highest culmination of desire for liberation, even desire for liberation itself must be abandoned.

  

This very principle is also sung by the Aṣṭāvakra Gītā: “The deluded person does not attain peace because he desires to quieten the mind. But the wise one, having ascertained the Truth, remains ever of peaceful mind.”
