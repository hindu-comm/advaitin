When we have different cognitions and consider them all as belonging to (or denoting/pointing to) the same thing/self, we are so to speak singularizing all these dualistic different nama-rupas in an unchanging nondual adhishtanam.

  

So a person sees a chair in different angles and thinks "same chair", sees a friend after 10 years and thinks "same friend", sees Vishnu in different temples in different poses, and maybe in different avataras as well, and thinks "same Narayana".

  

In each of these cases, a same self is identified in different cognitions. Or the cognitions are singularized as appearances pertaining to a single self.

  

However this is not a universal singularization of all cognitions in non-dual Self. The ishvara-jiva-jagat manifestation, we initially know it as an infinity of distinct selves, identities and (for astikas) their ishvara, each associated with their defining adjuncts, related to and interacting with each other. The same chair may be singularized in a series of cognitions but it will be considered different from a table. Table is one thing stable unto itself, chair is another. You are one jiva, I am another. The upadhi manifestations are well defined in vyavaharika and therefore seeing a multiplicity of selves with reference to different upadhi identifications is also natural.

  

So when Advaita says the Self/I is nondual, it is fundamentally overturning our dvaitic perspectives on reality. It is beckoning us to singularize our cognitions of table, of chair, of "table near/and chair", and every other possibility,as the "same I". What becomes of vyavaharika satya? It is no longer a duality of ishvara-jiva-jagat but the singular Ishvara, the One/"antaryamin" who appears as if many. What is the relation of the many to Ishvara? It is by way of His shakti/Maya, that He projects/imagines/cognizes Himself as many.

  

And when Advaita adds that the Self is nirguna, the relational-duality inherent in the knowledge of Self as Ishvara also reduces to adhyasa on Brahman. Thus Ishvara though the satya of vyavaharika (the plane of cognition) isasat in paramarthika, therefore (with reference to paramartha jnana) can be ascertained as mithya.

  

But the point I was making was that between the vyavaharika world based in the duality of ishvara-jiva-jagat and the paramarthika satya that is nirguna Brahman, there is the valid "connecting" step shown by shastra that singularizes all the dualistic cognitions in the nondual Self, i.e. we reduce to Ishvara (who yet is not paramarthika nirguna satya). As I said before, there can be a question of whether the affirmation of Ishvara is adhyaropa or a necessary logical limit to vyavaharika once we learn of Brahman. But seeing Brahman in the relational lens (as the singular Knower, Ishvara, etc.) is part of sampradaya imo. (If people think this is obvious, then my post can be taken as rhetorical.)

I think there may be a slight mutual misunderstanding.  
  
Regarding the Sanskrit verse from BUB 3-5-1 that you quoted, here is the translation for reference:  
  
"“Śoka and moha” — Śoka means desire (kāma). When one thinks about a desired object, the pleasure or attraction (ramaṇa) that arises with regard to that object becomes, in one overwhelmed by craving (tṛṣṇā), the seed of desire (kāma-bīja). Indeed, through that, desire becomes intensified. Moha, on the other hand, is non-discrimination (aviveka) arising from an erroneous cognition (viparīta-pratyaya); it is delusion (bhrama). And this \[delusion\] is ignorance (avidyā), the seed from which all misfortune or suffering (anartha) arises."  
  
I was not entirely clear on how this specific context applies to my point, so let me elaborate on my thought process.  
  
When we have different cognitions and recognize them as belonging to or pointing to the same Self, we are effectively singularizing these distinct nāma-rūpas into an unchanging, non-dual adhiṣṭhāna. For instance, seeing a chair from different angles and recognizing it as the "same chair," or seeing different forms/avatars and recognizing them as the "same Narayana," involves identifying a single underlying reality across varied cognitions.  
  
However, this is not automatically a universal singularization. In vyavahāra, the īśvara-jīva-jagat manifestation is initially experienced as a multiplicity of distinct entities and individuals, each tied to specific upādhis.  
  
When Advaita reveals that the Self is non-dual, it fundamentally transforms this perspective. It prompts us to resolve all distinct cognitions into the "same Self." In this view, vyavahāra is no longer seen merely as an unintegrated multiplicity, but as the singular Īśvara—the Antaryāmin—who appears as many through Māyā.  
  
Furthermore, when Advaita clarifies that the Self is nirguṇa, the relational duality inherent in conceiving the Self as Īśvara is recognized as adhyāsa on Brahman. Thus, while Īśvara serves as the reality (satya) of vyavahāra, from the stand-point of paramārtha, Īśvara as a relational entity is ultimately mithyā.  
  
My main point was that between the vyāvahārika view of a fragmented world and the pāramārthika truth of nirguṇa Brahman, the śāstra provides a valid intermediary step: singularizing all dualistic cognitions into the non-dual Self via Īśvara. Whether one views the affirmation of Īśvara as adhyāropa or as a logical necessity once Brahman is introduced, viewing Brahman through this relational lens is, in my view, an integral part of the sampradāya.
