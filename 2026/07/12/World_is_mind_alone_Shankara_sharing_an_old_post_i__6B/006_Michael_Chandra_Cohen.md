Namaste Sudhanshu ji,

Thank you — this is the right passage to put on the table, and I don't want to soften what it grants. Śrī SSS, glossing Alātaśānti 63–66, accepts the ācārya's anumāna in full: the waking jīvas are non-different from the citta that perceives them. There is no evading it, and I won't try. The bhāṣya you cite is exact:

*jāgrato dṛśyā jīvāḥ tac-cittāvyatiriktāḥ, cittekṣaṇīyatvāt, svapnadṛk-cittekṣaṇīya-jīvavat* — "The jīvas seen by the waking man are non-different from his citta, because they are objects perceived by citta, like the jīvas perceived by the dream-seer's citta."

Granted, and gladly. But the sentence does not stop there, and its second limb is the whole point:

*tac ca jīvekṣaṇātmakaṃ cittaṃ draṣṭur avyatiriktaṃ draṣṭṛ-dṛśyatvāt svapna-cittavat* — "And that citta, whose nature is the perceiving of those jīvas, is non-different from the *seer*, because it is a seen-object *of the seer*, like the dream-citta."

SSS's own vivṛti drives this home and then draws the conclusion the interlocutor's reading cannot absorb. On the waking citta he writes that it *tad-dṛśyam eva jāgrad-draṣṭur avyatiriktam ity abhyupagantavyam* — "is itself merely a seen-thing, to be admitted as non-different from the waking seer." And the section-heading on the following page states the terminus outright:

*citta-dṛśyavac cittam api naivāsti paramārthataḥ* — "Like the objects-seen-by-citta, **the citta too does not exist in reality**."

And the closing gloss: *jāgrataḥ puruṣasya cittaṃ tataḥ pṛthaṅ na vidyate, yasmāt tasmān na jāyate cittam* — "The waking person's citta does not exist apart from that \[seer\]; and since it does not, therefore citta is not born." The chapter's own summary word is *draṣṭur ekatvam* — the oneness of the **seer** — not the reality of the mind.

So notice the direction of the argument. It is a two-step dissolution: the seen world resolves into citta (*cittekṣaṇīyatvāt*), and citta in turn resolves into the draṣṭā (*draṣṭṛ-dṛśyatvāt*) — at which point citta is declared *naivāsti paramārthataḥ*. The "world is citta" clause is a *rung*, not the *ground*. What stands at the end is not mind but the sākṣin; citta is thrown in among its own objects as equally *asat*.

This is exactly why your own careful insistence — that *citta* here is not ātman, since it is *dṛśya* of the draṣṭā — tells against the mind-alone reading, not for it. Precisely because citta is *dṛśya*, it is *jaḍa*, *asat*, *na jāyate*. Its being was never its own; it is the draṣṭā's throughout. To read *tac-cittāvyatiriktāḥ* as "the mind is the being-source of the world" is to keep, as a standing ground, the very citta the passage exists to abolish.

Hence there is no contradiction to reconcile, because two different propositions are being run together under one phrase:

\(i\) The world is non-different from the seeing, and *at the limit both the seen and the seeing (citta) are asat, the draṣṭā alone real.* This is dṛg-dṛśya-viveka. SSS affirms it here and has never denied it anywhere; it is his method.

\(ii\) The citta / antaḥkaraṇa is the positive, *bhāvarūpa* upādāna of the world — a mind-substance that transforms into and sustains it. This is what SSS denies.

The passage you cite establishes (i) and, by negating citta (*cittam api naivāsti*), refutes (ii). When SSS says the world is "not mind," he is denying (ii): the world has no real mind-*upādāna*. He is not denying (i). Once the two senses of *citta-mātra* are held apart — the dissolvent and the constitutive — the appearance of self-contradiction dissolves along with the citta.

Put plainly: this text does not leave us with *cittam eva* as the surviving reality. It leaves us with *cittam api naivāsti* — and the draṣṭā alone. "Mind alone," read as a final ontology, is the one thing these four kārikās were written to remove.

praṇāms, Michael
