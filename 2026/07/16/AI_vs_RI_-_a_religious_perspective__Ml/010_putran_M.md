Namaskaram,

  

Chitta-ji pointed out that he had addressed these questions in his book Natural Realism and Contact theory of perception, "in Chapter 2 under the section*Refutation of Intentionalism*(pages 104 - 122)". Here are the purvapakshaarguments and their negation that he outlines there and elaborates further on.

  

1.If it is said that the representational states in the physical brain are deployed just as they are deployed in computing machines, then the reply is that even in the case of computing machines, what exists in them are mere physical states and not representations, and these physical states attain the status of representations only by virtue of them being so conceived by conscious agents. Just as words by themselves are mere sound, consisting of a sequence of phonemes, and attain their status as representative-signs (words) by virtue of them operating as signs in the cognitions of conscious agents, even so do all other kinds of representations.

2.If it is claimed that there is cognitive activity taking place in the brain in the same way that it takes place in a computing machine, then the reply is that there is no cognitive activity taking place in a computing machine and the cognitive activity imputed to it is on account of the meanings that are superimposed on the physical operations of the machine by conscious agents.

3.There can be no cognitive activity in a computing machine because it lacks the essential characteristic that makes a cognitive activity into a cognitive activity, namely self- awareness.

  

4.If it is claimed, on the basis of observation of certain behavioral patterns, that thinking can be an unconscious activity, the reply is that it cannot be so because such a locution would amount to a misuse of language. Also, the behavioral patterns that Intentionalists cite to justify their thesis are fully explicable through the dispositions caused by the knowledge that has been acquired in the past, so there is no necessity to posit the thesis of unconscious thinking.

  

5.And even as there is no cognitive activity in a computing machine on account of there being no self-awareness of the activity, even so in theIntentionalist’sexplanation of perception there is no self-awareness of what the Intentionalist considers to be perception; hence the explanation fails to be an explanation of perception.

  

6.If, in order to escape the problems that Intentionalism leads to when attempting to explain perception in purely naturalistic terms, the Intentionalist should argue that consciousness, along with the distinctive phenomenological features that are seen in perception, is produced, or emerges, from brain-processes, then all the arguments that we had brought to bear against Representationalism (in our section on Refutation of Representationalism) would become applicable to Intentionalism too and it would stand refuted thereby.
