The Arrival of Viśvāmitra:  
  
At this time sage Viśvāmitra arrived there. Then, the king Dasaratha having offered Sage Viśvāmitra Padya and Arghya. (water for washing feet  
and respectful offerings as a traditional ritual of honour to a guest). Subdued and with folded hands he (Dasharatha) spoke thus to Viśvāmitra:  
“Oh! Sage! Having come here, you, who are supremely pious and virtuous, have become worthy of veneration by me. What is your supreme wish?  
What shall I do for you?”  
  
Having heard the words of the king, Viśvāmitra spoke to him thus: “I practice religious discipline. For its fulfillment you should spare me  
your son, the noble-minded Rama of proven valour, who is capable of destroying the Rakshasa (demon) causing obstruction to me.”  
  
Vasishtha told these words to the king who was troubled on hearing this from Viśvāmitra. “If you do not keep your words, who else will honour them?”  
  
When Vasishtha told thus, Viśvāmitra heard from Rama’s men: “The Lotus-eyed Rama, after returning from his pilgrimage, has become depressed  
and remains silent and alone, holding everything as unreal”, and spoke as follows:  
  
He is entitled to study this scripture, who has the firm belief, “I am bound; let me be liberated”, who is not much ignorant and not wise either.  
He does his daily duties through our effort and appeal, with a gloomy face; or he does not do them. The prince is dispirited in duties like bath,  
worship of god, giving gifts and eating. He does not eat food until satisfaction (is obtained) even if requested. He also does not entertain/make  
laugh himself with playful sports in the courtyard, with swings operated by the passionate women from the harem, as the cataka bird (plays) with  
the shower of rain. King! The row of golden bracelets and armlets, set with bud-like rubies, does not gladden him, as the heaven (does not gladden)  
one whose concern is the fall (therefrom). He becomes extremely dejected at the sights of sporting maidens, at the winds blowing through the flowers  
at the homes girdled by creepers. Whichever object is proper, sweet, lovely and fascinating, even by that he is wearied, as it were, with eyes  
full of tears. He finds fault with beautiful women at dancing pastimes (saying): “Why do these shining women of the city give pain?” He does not  
rejoice in blameless food, couch, vehicle, sport, bathing or seat like one of drunken behaviour.
