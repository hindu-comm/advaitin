8. Verdict

The corpus-wide evidence decisively rejects a causally thin Sureśvara. His ignorance can be seed, power, material cause, and the source of transformation. It accounts for buddhi, duality, bondage, latency, and renewed manifestation. The positive-avidyā tradition preserves this side of the evidence and can claim genuine Sureśvarian ancestry.

But ancestry is not identity. TV II.178-180 permits the name avidyā to extend to what ignorance manifests while stating that it has no rūpa beyond non-apprehension of the Self. BUBV 4.3.1784 calls Self-ignorance a power, but 4.3.1787 concludes that the Self itself as unknown is called that power. NS III.7-8 answers the objection from causal efficacy through ajñāta Sat, not by declaring an independently positive ignorance-principle. BUBV 4.4.178-179 denies the need for another cause beyond the Self as unknown. Most importantly, BUBV 1.4.419-443 accepts the need for a cause of error while refusing to establish a distinctive intrinsic form of moha and grounding the empirical standing of ignorance and error in Sat.

These are not isolated concessions to be outweighed by counting causal predicates. They are Sureśvara's own answers when the proposed cause is pressed for a determinate nature or an existent ground. They therefore deserve interpretive priority.

The evidence favors Satchidanandendra on this precise but decisive point. Sureśvara repeatedly terminates the causal inquiry in non-recognition of the Self or in the Self and Reality as unknown. These expressions do not turn non-recognition into the bearer of properties; they prevent the causal description from separating itself from the Reality being misapprehended. The later positive-avidyā tradition is not thereby discredited. It develops possibilities that Sureśvara genuinely opened and gives them a phenomenal-causal, quasi-ontological articulation consistent with its own insistence that avidyā is anirvacanīya, neither sat nor asat. What it should not claim without further argument is simple identity between that developed structure and Sureśvara's own formulation.

The most accurate historical formula is therefore:

Sureśvara makes the empirical order causally intelligible through non-recognition; later Advaita increasingly gives ignorance phenomenal-causal, quasi-ontological articulation.

Sureśvara is a hinge not because he stands halfway between a noncausal theory of absence and a causal theory of positive ignorance. He already gives ignorance extensive causal density. He is a hinge because that density makes later systematization possible while his explicit analyses refuse to leave an ignorance-entity standing apart from non-apprehension and the unknown Self. PSA is right about causal inheritance. SSS is decidedly closer to Sureśvara's answer when the inherited causal language is pressed for its final meaning.
