Q1: When bheema meets hanuman, does he say paramatma is nirguna and can only be realised through jnana? \[The upanyasakar has interpreted with \*only\*\]

  

A1:

  

Yes. In the Mahabharata, when Bhima meets Hanuman, Bhima explicitly says that the Paramatma is nirguna and can be known through jnana. The passage occurs in the Vana Parva, in the episode of Bhima and Hanuman's encounter.

  

The relevant Sanskrit verse is:

  

\> निर्गुणः परमात्मा तु देहं व्याप्यावतिष्ठते।तमहं ज्ञानविज्ञेयं नावमन्ये न लङ्घये॥

  

Transliteration:

  

Nirguṇaḥ paramātmā tu dehaṃ vyāpya avatiṣṭhate,

  

Tam ahaṃ jñāna-vijñeyaṃ nāvamanye na laṅghaye.

  

Meaning:

  

“The Supreme Self, devoid of qualities, pervades the body and abides within it. I cannot disrespect Him, who is to be known through knowledge; therefore, I will not step over you.”

  

\### What is the philosophical significance?

  

There are three important points:

  

\* Nirguna Paramatma: Bhima explicitly describes the Supreme Self as nirguna — without attributes or qualities.

  

\* Knowledge as the means of realization: The expression jñāna-vijñeyam means that the Supreme Self is to be known through knowledge. This supports a jnana-oriented interpretation of the passage.

  

\* The divine presence in all beings: Bhima refuses to step over Hanuman because he recognizes the presence of the Supreme Self in the body.

  

One qualification is important: the verse says that Paramatma is knowable through jnana. It does not, by itself, establish that jnana is the only possible means of realization, to the exclusion of bhakti or other spiritual disciplines. That stronger claim would require interpretation in the context of a broader philosophical tradition.

  

You can read the Sanskrit and English translation in the \[Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Bhima–Hanuman dialogue\]([https://www.rahular.com/itihasa/gen/mahabharata/vol-ii/147.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com](https://www.rahular.com/itihasa/gen/mahabharata/vol-ii/147.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)).

  

  

Q2: How do dvaitins and visishtadvaitins interpret this, when they don't agree paramatma is nirguna?

  

A2:

  

The apparent contradiction is real, and it raises an important question: if Bhima calls Paramatma nirguna, how can Dvaita and Vishishtadvaita commentators accept the verse while maintaining that Vishnu possesses infinite auspicious qualities?

  

The key is that the three Vedanta traditions do not give the word nirguna the same meaning.

  

\* Advaita: ultimately, Brahman is without attributes or distinctions.

  

\* Vishishtadvaita: Brahman is free from all undesirable or material qualities, but possesses infinitely many auspicious qualities.

  

\* Dvaita: Vishnu is free from defects and possesses infinite auspicious qualities; nirguna cannot mean that he lacks all qualities.

  

There is an important distinction, however: explaining how a school could interpret this verse consistently with its philosophy is not the same as establishing the precise interpretation given by a particular traditional commentator.

  

\### 1\\ The Vishishtadvaita interpretation

  

For Ramanuja's school, the usual interpretation is that nirguna means devoid of defective qualities, not devoid of every quality. Brahman possesses infinite auspicious qualities, such as knowledge, power, compassion and bliss.

  

Applied to Bhima's statement, a Vishishtadvaitin could reason as follows:

  

\* Nirgunaḥ paramātmā — Paramatma is free from the defective qualities associated with finite, material existence.

  

\* Jñāna-vijñeyam — Paramatma is to be known through spiritual knowledge and realization.

  

\* Dehaṃ vyāpya avatiṣṭhate — the Supreme Self pervades the body.

  

The third point is compatible with Vishishtadvaita's understanding of God as the inner ruler (antaryāmin) of all beings.

  

However, there is a textual qualification: the Sanskrit word nirguṇa does not itself explicitly say “free from defective qualities.” That is the school's philosophical interpretation of the word in light of its broader understanding of scripture.

  

\### 2\\ The Dvaita interpretation

  

Madhva's Dvaita Vedanta likewise rejects the idea that Vishnu lacks all attributes. Vishnu possesses infinite auspicious qualities and is eternally distinct from individual souls. Dvaita interpreters understand scriptural denials of qualities in relation to material or limiting qualities, rather than as denying all divine attributes.

  

A Dvaitin could therefore read Bhima's statement this way:

  

\* Paramatma is nirguna because he is untouched by the three material gunas and their defects.

  

\* He is knowable through jnana, but this knowledge does not make the individual soul identical with God.

  

\* God's presence within a body does not mean that God and the embodied individual are one and the same entity.

  

The last point matters especially in Dvaita: God remains independent, while the individual soul remains dependent on him.

  

As with Vishishtadvaita, this is a reconstruction consistent with the school's principles; I have not verified a specific Dvaita commentary on this particular verse.

  

\### 3\\ But is that really what Bhima's words mean?

  

This is the most interesting question.

  

The verse says:

  

\> Nirguṇaḥ paramātmā tu … jñāna-vijñeyam.

  

Read in isolation, nirguṇa can naturally suggest “without qualities,” and jñāna-vijñeyam suggests “knowable through knowledge.” A reader might therefore see it as supporting an Advaita-like understanding.

  

The theistic Vedanta schools, however, interpret individual scriptural statements within a larger body of texts that describe God as possessing infinite qualities. They do not accept that this single expression establishes the Advaita doctrine of an ultimately attributeless Brahman.

  

There is also a distinction between what a verse can mean within a philosophical system and what its author most likely intended in its immediate context. Establishing the latter requires examining the surrounding Sanskrit and the traditional commentaries, not merely applying a school's general doctrine.

  

For comparison, see the discussions of nirguna in \[Vishishtadvaita\]([https://cs.rkmvu.ac.in/~tamal/learn/iks/darshanas/vedanta/visistadvaita.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com](https://cs.rkmvu.ac.in/~tamal/learn/iks/darshanas/vedanta/visistadvaita.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) and \[Dvaita\]([https://ebooks.inflibnet.ac.in/icp03/chapter/dvaita/?utm_source=chatgpt.com](https://ebooks.inflibnet.ac.in/icp03/chapter/dvaita/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)).

  

My conclusion: Both schools have a coherent way to reconcile the verse with their theology. But unless we locate a traditional commentary specifically addressing Bhima's words, we should not claim that either Madhva or Ramanuja explicitly interpreted this particular verse in that way.
