namaste sudhanshuji,  

### Gauḍapāda makes your issue sharper. At Kārikā 4.64, Gauḍapāda says: 

### स्वप्नदृक्चित्तदृश्यास्ते न विद्यन्ते ततः पृथक् । The beings perceived in dream do not exist separately from the dreamer's mind.

Then, at 4.66, he applies the same reasoning to waking:  
जाग्रच्चित्तेक्षणीयास्ते न विद्यन्ते ततः पृथक् ।  
The beings perceived in waking do not exist separately from the waking mind.

I consulted SSS and found a remarkably explicit answer in SSS's The Essential Gauḍapāda. He expressly rejects interpreting Gauḍapāda 4.64ff as establishing ekajīva-vāda. His explanation also clarifies why the waking–dream parallel should not be converted into a theory of creation by one perceiving jīva.

1. The Essential Gauḍapāda, pp. 490–494

SSS fully accepts the radical meaning of Kārikās 4.64–66. Dream beings have no existence apart from the dreamer's mind, and the same reasoning applies to beings perceived in waking.

But he immediately takes the argument one step further.

On p. 491, he explains that the waking mind itself has no independent existence apart from the Ātman that illuminates it. Thus the reasoning does not stop at the mind as the producer of appearances. It negates the independent existence of both mind and its objects.

This is reinforced by Kārikā 4.67, which SSS interprets as establishing that citta and cetya (mind and what is mentally apprehended) are mutually dependent. Neither can be independently established as the ultimately existing reality.

Then comes the decisive passage.

The Essential Gauḍapāda, p. 493

HERE THERE IS NO SCOPE FOR INTRODUCING THE EKAJĪVAVĀDA

SSS explains that the expression sarve jīvāḥ in Kārikās 4.68–70 cannot mean all the other jīvas except the waking jīva.

Why? Because Kārikā 4.71 explicitly declares:

न कश्चिज्जायते जीवः सम्भवोऽस्य न विद्यते ।  
“No jīva whatsoever is ever born; there is no origination of it.”

SSS's argument is that the purpose of the entire passage is to deny the ultimate reality of jīva-plurality, not to reduce many empirical jīvas to one ultimately privileged empirical jīva.

2. Māṇḍūkya Rahasya Vivṛtti, pp. 214–215

SSS writes:

न चावस्थात्रयस्यापि केनचिदेकेन चित्तेनानुस्यूतेन सम्बन्धः कार्यकारणभावो वा शक्यकल्पनः ।  
“Nor can one reasonably suppose that the three states are connected, or related as cause and effect, through one continuous mind.”

SSS observes that the waking mind and dream mind are experienced differently, each with its own objects and spatiotemporal conditions. In deep sleep, even those conditions are absent.

His immediate target is the Vijñānavādin's assumption of an underlying continuity of mind. We should not confuse that doctrine with Madhusūdana's ekajīva-vāda. Nevertheless, the argument prevents us from treating the waking–dream comparison as proof of one continuously functioning mind that creates both worlds.

More importantly, SSS states on p. 215:

अजाद्वयात्मव्यतिरेकेण तत्स्वतन्त्रं नास्त्येव ।

“The \[apparent differentiation\] has no independent existence apart from the unborn, nondual Self.”

He explains that the negation of waking and dreaming does not mean simply that their contents are present in those states and absent in deep sleep. The point is their absence of independent reality apart from Ātman.

Thus the argument is directed toward Ātman as the reality of all three states, rather than toward establishing a productive relationship between a single mind and its objects.

3. What SSS is actually doing

I think the distinction is now quite clear.

The DSV interpretation: Since dream objects exist only as appearances to the dreamer's mind, waking objects likewise exist only through the perception of the single jīva. The world is consequently explained through that jīva's ignorance.

SSS's interpretation: Since both mind and its objects are themselves appearances, neither possesses independently established reality. Their dependence points beyond both to the Self that illuminates them.

The dream analogy therefore serves to negate the apparent independence of the entire knower–known relation, including the empirical knower.

This is reinforced elsewhere in The Essential Gauḍapāda, p. 140, where SSS rejects the post-Śaṅkara controversy over whether Brahman or the jīva is the agent of imagination. He maintains that Gauḍapāda's purpose is not to identify a metaphysical agent of imagination, but to establish that duality is unreal and nondual Ātman alone is real.

The argument begins with dream objects being dependent on the dreamer's mind, extends the same dependence to waking objects, then denies independent reality to the mind itself, finally concluding that no jīva whatsoever is born.

The progression is:

4.64–66 — Mind and perceived objects are not independently established

4.67 — Their existence is mutually dependent

4.68–70 — The apparent birth of all jīvas is unreal

4.71 — No jīva whatsoever is born

The conclusion is ajāti, not ekajīva-vāda.
